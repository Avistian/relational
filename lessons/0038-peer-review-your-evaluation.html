Peer Review Your Evaluation

Lesson 038 · Phase 1 · Q4 · ~50 min · primary reading: Lones, Patterns 5(10), 2024

Two lessons ago you put on a mechanic's overalls and audited your own homework pipeline for whether the number was right (L036). Last lesson you put on an engineer's and asked whether it was stable — whether the pipeline regenerates its own headline on command (L037). This lesson hands you a third hat, and it is the uncomfortable one: the reviewer's. Someone drops an evaluation on your desk — a report, a results table, a claim that model A beats model B — and asks the only question that ultimately decides whether the work counts: would this survive a hostile, competent reader who is not you?

This is not the same skill as auditing your own code, and the difference is the point. An auditor has the source; a reviewer usually has only what was reported, and must decide what to believe from it. An auditor lists defects; a reviewer must triage them — separate the flaw that changes the conclusion from the flaw that merely dents the artifact — and end on a verdict with required changes, not a pile of grievances. Doing that fairly, under a fixed checklist, is a distinct and learnable craft, and it is the craft the whole mission runs on.

Its single skill: run a structured, severity-graded review of an evaluation across three axes — leakage, tuning, metrics — and reach a defensible verdict that names what must change, while holding the baseline to exactly the standard you would demand of a result that beat you.

One sentence. A peer review is not a list of everything wrong; it is a sorted judgement — which defects invalidate the conclusion, which merely degrade the artifact, and which are nits — delivered as a verdict a fair author could act on, and its hardest discipline is applying the same scrutiny to the baseline you want to lose.

Why review at all — and why it is the mission's immune system

The mission's bet is contrarian: that relational deep learning is undervalued, and the way that bet is settled is a comparison a skeptic will attack — an RDL model against a tuned gradient-boosted tree with hand-built features, on RelBench, with numbers a reviewer will try to break. So the reviewer you are most afraid of is the one you must become, twice over. First, because you cannot pre-empt an attack you cannot perform: to publish a win that holds, you have to review your own result as its harshest reader would. Second — and this is the subtler discipline — because the claim is comparative, it needs two pipelines reviewed to the same standard, and the one you are least motivated to scrutinise is the GBDT baseline you are hoping to beat. A generous review of your model and a lazy review of the baseline is how honest people publish false wins. The checklist exists to make the standard the same for both, whatever you are rooting for.

This is not an abstract worry. Kapoor & Narayanan (whose leakage taxonomy you met in L022) found data leakage across 17 fields and 329 papers, and in their own civil-war reproduction, every claim that a complex model beat logistic regression evaporated once the leak was removed. Those papers were reviewed — by competent people — and the flaws got through. Peer review is the field's immune system, and like any immune system it only works if the antibodies are specific: a vague "seems fine" catches nothing. This lesson gives you the specific antibodies, organised as a checklist.

First, the vocabulary (nothing here is assumed)

A review has its own small language, and using it precisely is most of what separates a useful review from a taste-based one. Everything this lesson relies on is defined here from first principles.

The reviewer's stance. You review the reported evaluation, not the author's intentions or their character. Two consequences. You must reason from what is on the page plus what a competent author would have had to do — so omissions are findings: "the split is not described" is itself a defect, because an undescribed choice cannot be trusted. And you extend the author no benefit of the doubt you would not extend to a rival's paper. The stance is adversarial toward the claim and generous toward the person.
Blocker, major, minor, nit. The four severities a review sorts findings into. A blocker means the central claim is not supported as written — you cannot recommend acceptance until it is resolved. A major issue materially weakens the work but leaves the headline standing. A minor issue should be fixed but changes no conclusion. A nit is cosmetic. The single most common review failure is grading everything the same — a wall of nits reads as thoroughness and buries the one blocker that mattered.
Conclusion-impact vs artifact severity. The two independent axes this lesson turns on. Conclusion-impact: if the defect were fixed, would the paper's claim (or the shipped decision) change? Artifact severity: how broken is the thing itself, regardless of the headline? They come apart in both directions — a group-straddle buried in a training block can leave the reported number perfectly honest (high severity, low conclusion-impact), while a 0.0032-nat selection margin can decide everything while every number is correctly measured (low severity, high conclusion-impact). Grading only one axis is how reviewers either nuke a sound paper over a cosmetic flaw or wave through a fatal one dressed as a small number.
Verdict. The single required output of a review, in four grades: accept (claims supported), minor revision (fixable without changing the story), major revision (the conclusion is overstated but the work is salvageable with named changes), reject (the central claim is unsupported and cannot be rescued by revision). A review without a verdict is a mood; a verdict without required changes is a veto. You owe both.
Sequential overfitting (over-hyping). Lones' name for one of the most pernicious leaks, because it is almost never written down: you train model after model, using each one's test-set score to choose the next, so the test set silently becomes part of training and the final number is optimistic. It is the informal cousin of the selection bias L004 fixed with nested CV — and a reviewer catches it not in the code but in the process: "how many models did you evaluate on this test set, and did any earlier result guide a later choice?"
Hyper-parameter (HP) budget parity. A fair comparison optimises every compared model to the same degree. If your new model had a 200-trial search and the baseline ran on defaults, the gap you measured is a tuning-effort gap wearing a model-quality mask (Lones: "do not assume a bigger number means a better model"). The reviewer's question is not "did you tune?" but "did you tune both, equally, and report the budget?" — which is exactly why L024 reports a budget curve rather than one peak.
Multiplicity effect (multiple comparisons). Each significance test at 95 % confidence has a 1-in-20 chance of a false positive; run twenty and one is likely spurious. A benchmark that compares many models on one test set, or a community that compares thousands on a shared benchmark, is running exactly this gauntlet — so a small "win" is the expected behaviour of noise, not evidence. The fixes are a correction (Bonferroni/Holm) and refusing to read significance into a marginal lead. You met the single-dataset version in L023.
Meaningful vs naive baseline. A comparison is only informative against the right floor. A naive (dummy) baseline — predict the majority class, predict last-value — proves the model does something non-trivial at all. A meaningful baseline is the strong, established approach a skeptic would reach for (here: a tuned GBDT, and per L028 a tuned MLP/ResNet). Beating a weak baseline proves nothing; Lones' rule is to freshly implement and equally tune every model you compare.
Significance vs effect size. Two different questions. Significance asks "is the difference distinguishable from noise?"; effect size asks "is it big enough to care?" With enough samples a trivial difference becomes significant, and with few samples a real difference is not — so a review demands both a correct test (L023) and the raw magnitude, and treats "p<0.05" with no effect size, or a mean gap with no test, as half an answer.
Model info sheet / REFORMS. Two checklists this lesson's checklist descends from. Kapoor & Narayanan's model info sheet (L022) forces one leakage question per leak type before publication. REFORMS (Kapoor, Cantrell, … Lones, Malik et al., 2024) is a consensus-based reporting checklist for ML-based science. A checklist's value is that it makes you answer a question rather than assume it — which is the entire mechanism by which review catches what taste misses.

The evaluation under review

Rather than review a toy, we review the artifact you already know inside-out: the ReAction L&D response-prediction submission in ~/Projects/homework, whose report.md makes three headline claims. Treat it now as a stranger's paper — the fact that you have the source is a luxury a real reviewer rarely has, so we will lean on reported evidence and use the source only to confirm. The claims:

  1. "M2a (LightGBM + isotonic calibration) is our best model," selected by lowest mean cross-validated log-loss.
  2. "Calibration is good: ECE 0.018," reported in the README and the ship-gate table.
  3. "Reproducible: all randomness is seeded."

Everything the review cites below was measured in L036 (the audit) or L037 (the package). No new numbers appear in this lesson; the skill on show is not measuring, it is reading the existing measurements as a reviewer — sorting them by the two axes and turning them into a verdict.

Axis 1 — the leakage sweep

Leakage is first because it is the failure that most often makes a good number a lie, and because it has a structure you can sweep rather than hope to notice. A reviewer does not "look for leaks"; they walk the eight-type taxonomy (Kapoor & Narayanan, L022) and, for each type, ask which reported sentence answers it — and treat silence as a finding. Click through the map and, for each, hold the homework submission against it.

Swept against the submission, the taxonomy comes back mostly clean, and saying so precisely is part of the review. L1.2 / L1.3 (preprocessing and feature selection on all data): the audit found a transductive fit — pipe.fit_transform(X_all) over all rows before the CV loop — so the reported CV number is not strictly a deployment estimate; a real but bounded major. L3.1 (temporal): the task is not timestamped in a way that supports a temporal split, so this is a declarable limitation, not a defect — the reviewer's note is "state that the split is random and why." L3.2 (non-independence): the outer CV is correctly person-grouped — credit where due — but the calibration wrapper takes an ungrouped inner split, a contained group-straddle we return to below.

The leak that does not inflate the number. The instinct — "there's a leak, so the number is inflated" — is wrong, and the review has to be precise about it. The ungrouped inner calibration split lives entirely inside the outer training block, so the outer test fold never enters the calibrator's fit: the reported log-loss (1.4248) and ECE (0.0363) are honest measurements of the model actually built. Re-measured with a grouped inner split, they move to 1.4232 / 0.0360 — the change is far inside the 0.039 fold σ, and it is in the correct direction (the honest number is slightly better, not worse). So this finding is high artifact severity (the shipped calibrator is mis-shaped for strangers) and low conclusion-impact (the reported number stands). Name the contaminated quantity before you estimate damage; "leak" does not imply "inflated."

Axis 2 — the tuning & selection sweep

The second axis is the one leakage checklists miss entirely, and where claim 1 lives. It asks whether the choice the evaluation made — which model won, and by how much it was tuned — is supported. Three questions.

Was the test set touched more than once (sequential overfitting)? A reviewer asks how many models were compared on the same folds and whether any earlier score guided a later decision. Here the answer is bounded — a fixed set of candidate models on shared grouped folds — but the shared-fold comparison sets up the real problem, the winner's curse.

Is the winning margin real? This is the blocker. The submission ships M2a over M1 on a mean-log-loss margin of 0.0032 nats — 8 % of one fold's σ, losing 2 of the 5 folds, with a naive paired p = 0.64 and a Nadeau–Bengio corrected p = 0.75 — and the winner flips to M1 the moment fold 2, which supplies M2a's entire margin, is dropped (L036). Every number here is correctly measured; the defect is the inference. The maximum of several noisy, correlated estimates is optimistically biased (the winner's curse, L036 / L017), so an argmin over shared folds is a decision, not a measurement — and a decision that flips when one fold is dropped was never a decision about the models. The reviewer's required change: give the tie to the simpler model, or report "no significant winner."

Was the comparison budget-fair? Here the axis borrows a finding from the reproducibility probe. An undocumented .astype(np.float32), sitting in a notebook cell mentioned in neither the README nor src/, moves the mean log-loss by only +0.00133 — but that is 42 % of the 0.0032 margin that decided claim 1 (L037). A representation choice nobody declared supplies nearly half the evidence for the headline. That is not a rounding detail; it is part of the model, and it belongs in the comparison's config where a reviewer can see it.

Axis 3 — the metrics sweep

The third axis asks whether the numbers mean what the report says they mean, and it is where claim 2 fails. Two findings, both from L037.

Is each number's estimator named? "Top-label ECE" on the same model's same out-of-fold predictions reads 0.0332 in the selection table (binned per fold, then averaged) and 0.018 in the README and ship-gate (pooled, binned once) — a 1.87× spread that says nothing about the model, because ECE sums absolute gaps over bins and thin bins bias it upward, while log-loss (a plain per-row mean) agrees to five decimals either way. The model was selected on the larger estimator and ship-gated on the smaller one. Neither is wrong; the defect is that a metric name is not an estimator, and until the aggregation rule, bin count and sample size travel with the number, the two are not comparable.

Is each threshold above its estimator's noise floor? The submission's one failed ship-gate — the 107-row age = missing slice, ECE 0.094 against a 0.05 threshold — sits below the 0.1071 ± 0.0297 that a perfectly calibrated model scores at n = 107 (L037). The gate cannot be passed by any model, so its CONDITIONAL verdict is uninterpretable in either direction. Before believing any small reported value, a reviewer asks what the same procedure would report for a model that was exactly right — and a threshold beneath its own floor is a coin flip with a procedure attached.

Triage: the two axes that decide the verdict

Now the skill this whole lesson is built to teach. You have a sweep's worth of findings; a novice review lists them and a good review sorts them, on the two independent axes from the vocabulary. Below, each defect from the three sweeps (plus the two reproducibility findings that back them) is placed by conclusion-impact (does fixing it change the claim?) against artifact severity (how broken is the thing itself?). Click each to see its evidence and the required change. Watch what the grid does that a list cannot: it puts the ungrouped split and the no-lockfile finding high but to the left — serious, but not why you would reject the conclusion — and the selection margin, the estimator ambiguity and the single fold draw to the right, where the claims actually live.

Read the right-hand column top to bottom and you have the review's spine: the three headline claims are each undercut by a right-side finding. That is the difference between "here are seven things I noticed" and "here is why I cannot accept the paper as written, and exactly what would change my mind."

Predict the verdict before you read it

Commit first. You have swept three axes and triaged the findings. What verdict does this evaluation earn?

Verdict · with required changes

Major revision — the machinery is sound; the three headline claims are overstated

Not a reject: the data, the grouped outer CV, the saved artifacts and the internal checks are better than most published ML, and every defect is fixable without redoing the study. Not an accept, because each of the three claims fails as written. Claim 1 ("M2a is best") rests on a 0.0032-nat margin that does not survive a corrected test and flips on dropping one fold (blocker) — and 42 % of it is an undeclared dtype cast. Required: report the tie, or justify M2a on grounds other than the mean. Claim 2 ("ECE 0.018") uses an unnamed estimator that disagrees 1.87× with the selection table's, and the one failed gate sits below its noise floor (blocker). Required: name the estimator everywhere and re-scope the sub-floor gate. Claim 3 ("reproducible") is true and uninformative — the seed is inert — and the pipeline ships no lockfile, so a reviewer cannot regenerate it (major). Required: a lockfile and a fingerprint gate. The submission is a revise, and a good one; the review's job is to make the write-up as honest as the engineering already is.

Explain it back

This is the load-bearing idea — put it in your own words before moving on.

Thesis bridge. Year 1 ends (L040) by asking you to beat XGBoost on a flat task or explain why not — and Years 3–4 ask the same question of RDL against a GBDT baseline on RelBench, where the leaderboard gaps you are trying to move are frequently a few percent. This lesson is the tool that decides whether such a gap is believable. The winner's-curse finding says a 0.0032-nat "win" is noise; the RelBench deltas you will chase are not always much larger, so the same corrected test and the same "report the tie" discipline are what will separate your real result from your enthusiasm. And the hardest half is the one the lesson keeps returning to: the review that makes an RDL win credible is the one where you audited the baseline — the model you wanted to lose — to exactly the standard you demanded of your own. A skeptic reading your paper will do precisely that; do it first.

Where this is subtle (know this too)

  • A review is triage, not enumeration. The output is a sorted judgement with a verdict, not a list. Seven findings with no severities is a worse review than three findings correctly graded, because the reader cannot tell the blocker from the nit — and burying one real blocker under a drift of cosmetic notes is how fatal flaws get waved through.
  • "Leak" does not imply "inflated number." Name the contaminated quantity first. A leak confined to the training block (the ungrouped calibrator) degrades the shipped artifact while leaving the reported metric honest — high artifact severity, low conclusion-impact. The reverse also happens: a correctly-measured 0.0032-nat margin decides everything while nothing is mismeasured.
  • Omissions are findings. "The estimator is not named," "the split is not described," "no lockfile" — an undescribed choice is an untrustworthy one, and a reviewer scores it as such rather than assuming the author did the right thing off-page.
  • Hold the baseline to the model's standard. The failure mode of an honest researcher is a generous review of the model they love and a lazy one of the baseline they want to beat. Equal HP budget, equal leakage sweep, equal significance test — whatever you are rooting for.
  • Significance and effect size are both required, and neither is enough alone. A corrected p with no magnitude cannot tell you if a real difference matters; a mean gap with no test cannot tell you if it is real. The winner's-curse blocker here needed both the corrected p=0.75 and the "flips on dropping one fold" magnitude to be damning.
  • Community benchmarks are a shared multiple-comparisons trap. Even if everyone uses the test set once, a thousand papers on one leaderboard means the top model is partly the luckiest (Lones; L024). A reviewer discounts a marginal SOTA lead accordingly — which is exactly the caution the RelBench leaderboard will demand of your own entry.
  • The verdict must be actionable and bounded. "Major revision" with named required changes is a service to the author; "reject, it feels weak" is a veto. Match the grade to the ceiling of what revision can fix: reject only when no revision rescues the claim.

Check your understanding

Primary reading (today)

Michael A. Lones, "Avoiding common machine learning pitfalls," Patterns 5(10):101046, 2024 (doi:10.1016/j.patter.2024.101046, open access; the continuously-updated preprint is arXiv:2108.02497, "How to avoid machine learning pitfalls: a guide for academic researchers"). It is a checklist by construction — a "dos and don'ts" tour of the ML lifecycle — and its middle three sections are this lesson's three axes: "How to robustly evaluate models" (leakage, sequential overfitting, choose metrics carefully, evaluate multiple times), "How to compare models fairly" (do not assume a bigger number means a better model, use meaningful baselines, statistical tests, correct for multiple comparisons), and "How to report your results" (be transparent, report performance multiple ways, be careful with significance, use an ML checklist).

Read it with one question in hand: for each "do not," which axis of your checklist catches it, and where has this course already shown it happening? Almost every item maps onto something you have measured — "do not assume a bigger number means a better model" is the 0.0032-nat winner's curse; "be careful when reporting statistical significance" is L023's corrected test; "choose metrics carefully" is the ECE estimator ambiguity. The items that do not map to something you have already met are the ones worth flagging for later.

Two companions if you have the appetite, both checklists your deliverable descends from. Kapoor & Narayanan's Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in ML-based science (Patterns, 2023) gives the eight-type taxonomy and the model info sheet — one leakage question per type. And REFORMS (Kapoor, Cantrell, Peng, … Lones, Malik et al., Science Advances, 2024) is the consensus reporting checklist the whole field is converging on; skim its item list and notice how much of it your three axes already cover.

Practice — review it as if it were a stranger's

The lab for this lesson is a review you write, not a notebook you run. Take the homework report.md (or, if you would rather review something you did not build, ask your teacher for a short evaluation write-up to critique) and produce a real review: sweep the three axes with the checklist above, grade each finding as blocker / major / minor / nit, place the two or three that matter on the conclusion-impact × artifact-severity grid, and end on a verdict with required changes. Then paste it into chat — your teacher will review your review, checking that you graded severity correctly, caught the blockers, held the baseline to the model's standard, and did not bury the one finding that changes the claim under a drift of nits.

Unsure why a leak can leave the reported number honest, when "major revision" is the right verdict rather than "reject," or how to hold a baseline you want to beat to the same standard as the model you love?
