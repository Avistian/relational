Mission · RDL thesis · Year 1 · Q4

Year 1 Synthesis Essay

Lesson 039 · Phase 1 · Q4 · ~55 min · primary reading: Grinsztajn, Oyallon & Varoquaux, NeurIPS 2022 (full)

Thirty-eight lessons ago you met the single-table assumption. Since then you have built a leakage spine, tuned gradient-boosted trees into a stubborn incumbent, measured why they win on flat tables, watched every single-table upgrade — neural nets, AutoML, embeddings, attention, hand-crafted features — tie or fail, watched a flatten destroy recoverable signal, and turned the whole apparatus on your own homework until a peer-review verdict said major revision. This lesson asks you to do one new thing with all of that: write the argument.

Not a diary of what you studied. Not a chronological tour. An essay with a claim, evidence, boundaries, and an honest open question — the artifact the Year 1 exit exam (L040, next) will either defend in an experiment or revise in public. The curriculum titles it plainly: what trees beat and why.

Its single skill: compose a synthesis essay that argues, with Year-1 evidence, what a competently-built tree ensemble beats on flat tabular tasks and why — naming the three inductive biases, the exhaustion of single-table cleverness, the conditions where the claim flips, and the relational frontier the flatten cannot speak to — ending in a claim a hostile reviewer could grade.

One sentence. A synthesis is an argument, not a recap: trees beat weak and undertuned alternatives on typical flat tables because their inductive biases match irregular targets, privileged columns, and junk features; they merely match honest neural nets and AutoML; and the claim stops being the whole story the moment the table was manufactured by a lossy join.

Warm up — recall before you write

Three questions from earlier in the year, from memory. A failed retrieval still encodes better than a re-read — and every answer below is a sentence your essay may need.

First, the vocabulary (what a synthesis is)

Before assembling Year 1, name the genre. A synthesis essay has its own small language, and using it precisely is most of what separates a useful one from a study guide.

Synthesis essay. A short written argument that assembles prior results into a single claim a reader can accept, reject, or revise — organised by the logic of the claim, not by the order you learned the pieces. Contrast a recap (chronological coverage) and a literature review (survey of others' claims). The deliverable of this lesson is a synthesis.
Claim. One sentence a hostile reader could falsify. Not "I learned a lot about trees," but "On typical medium-sized flat tabular tasks, a leak-free tuned GBDT matches or beats honest neural baselines because of three inductive biases, and the remaining unpaid upside sits across the relational join the flatten discarded."
Boundary condition. A stated regime where the claim is not supposed to hold. Without boundaries, "trees win" is a slogan; with them, it is a scientific sentence. Grinsztajn's own flips — smooth targets, rotated features, low junk — are boundary conditions. So is "after a lossy flatten."
Open burden. The part of the mission the essay must not pretend Year 1 has discharged. Here: no result yet shows a relational model recovering discarded structure to beat the fair flat bar. Naming the open burden is how a synthesis stays honest.
Evidence of record. A number or finding you are willing to cite because it was measured under a disclosed protocol in this course (or in a ★ paper), not recalled as folklore. Prefer the verified figures from L010 / L019–L030 / L031–L038 over vibes.

The claim this essay exists to make

Write it once, up front, so every later section has somewhere to point:

Working claim (edit it; do not abandon its shape). On typical flat tabular tasks — medium-sized tables whose columns carry individual meaning, whose targets are irregular, and whose feature sets contain junk — a leak-free, budget-tuned gradient-boosted tree matches or beats honest neural baselines and AutoML because its inductive biases fit that regime. Further single-table cleverness (deeper nets, embeddings, attention, hand-crafted features) has repeatedly tied or failed against that bar. The claim is conditional: it flips under smooth targets, rotated bases, and low-noise regimes — and it is silent on signal a lossy join already destroyed. Whether a model that keeps relational structure can beat this fair flat bar is the open burden of Years 3–6.

That paragraph is denser than it looks. It smuggles in the measurement contract ("leak-free, budget-tuned"), the opponent ("honest neural baselines and AutoML"), the mechanism ("inductive biases"), the exhaustion result ("tied or failed"), the boundaries ("flips under…"), and the thesis hinge ("lossy join" / "open burden"). Your essay's job is to unpack each of those into evidence — not to invent a different claim that Year 1 cannot support.

Section II — why (three inductive biases)

An inductive bias is the kind of function a learner reaches for before the data forces it elsewhere. A linear model reaches for flat boundaries; a neural net reaches for smooth ones; a decision tree reaches for axis-aligned, piecewise-constant ones. No bias is universally right — the question is whether it matches the data. Grinsztajn, Oyallon & Varoquaux (2022) benchmarked deep nets against XGBoost and Random Forests across dozens of datasets, then ran controlled experiments to find why the trees kept winning on medium-sized tabular data. They distilled three challenges for tabular nets — the mirror image of which is three tree strengths.

1 · Irregular (non-smooth) targets. Neural nets are biased toward smooth functions: small changes in x give small changes in the prediction. Many tabular targets are not — default risk jumps at an income threshold; a fee kicks in at day 30. A tree places a hard step; a smooth-biased net rounds it off. Ablation: smoothing the target collapses the GBT–MLP gap (L025).
2 · Orientation / privileged basis. Tree splits are axis-aligned, so they attend to each column as an individually meaningful coordinate. An MLP is rotationally invariant (W·(Qx)=(WQ)·x). A lossless random rotation collapses the tree and leaves the MLP unmoved, reversing the ranking (L026: tree 0.987→0.747, MLP 0.862→0.869). Invariance is a liability when columns carry individual meaning — the default for business tables, the opposite of image pixels.
3 · Robustness to uninformative features. Trees gate junk via greedy, gain-gated split selection (implicit feature selection). MLPs feed every column into layer 1 and, being rotation-invariant, need at least linearly more samples per junk feature (Ng 2004). On a smooth target where the MLP wins clean, adding 100 noise columns reverses the ranking (L027).

Section III — where the claim stops

A claim without boundaries is a slogan. Year 1 gives you three kinds of boundary, and the essay needs all three:

  1. Flip conditions inside the flat table. Smooth the target, rotate the basis, remove the junk — and the tree's edge shrinks or reverses. Say so.
  2. Exhaustion inside the flat table. Honest nets, AutoML, embeddings, attention, and hand FE repeatedly tie or fail against a tuned GBDT. "Try harder on one table" is not the unpaid upside.
  3. Silence about what the flatten destroyed. Ada and Bo collide into one feature row (L035); any model — tree or net — then predicts the same probability. Trees can only "win" on what survived the join. That is not a defence of flattening; it is the thesis's opening.

Section IV — the credibility coda

An essay that argues "trees beat X" without the evaluation apparatus is doing what Kapoor & Narayanan found in 329 papers: stating a ranking a leak or a winner's curse would dissolve. You have already lived this on your own submission (L036–L038):

  • A 0.0032-nat model selection that fails a corrected test (p=0.75) and flips on one dropped fold.
  • An ECE that is 0.0332 or 0.018 depending on an unnamed estimator.
  • A splitter seed that spans 0.0166 nats — 5× the selection margin.
  • A peer-review verdict of major revision, under a checklist that holds the baseline to the model's standard.

So the essay's last move is not a flourish — it is a constraint: every comparative sentence in this essay inherits the peer-review checklist. If you claim trees beat an MLP, you owe HP budget parity, a leakage sweep, a corrected test, and an effect size. If you later claim RDL beats trees, you owe the same to the GBDT. That discipline is the mission's immune system; the synthesis is where you put it in writing.

A skeleton you can fill (do not submit the skeleton)

Use this as scaffolding. Your voice, your sentences, your choice of which evidence of record to cite — but keep the shape. Length target: roughly 800–1,200 words (about 1.5–2 pages). Shorter is fine if every sentence earns its place; longer is fine if you are unpacking a number, not padding.

1. Claim (one paragraph)

State the working claim. Name the regime (flat, medium-sized, irregular, junk-heavy). Name the opponent (honest neural + AutoML). Name the open burden in one clause.

2. Measurement contract (short)

Leakage spine, metric of record, disclosed budget. Cite L010 / L020 / L023 / L038 as the standard you are holding yourself to.

3. What trees beat (evidence table in prose)

Walk the opponent ladder: dummy → weak net → honest net / AutoML → embeddings / TT / FE. Distinguish win from tie from within noise.

4. Why (three biases)

One short subsection each: irregular targets, orientation, junk features. One verified number each. One sentence on what would flip that bias.

5. Where the claim stops

Flat-table flip conditions; the exhaustion cascade; the aggregation collision and the four discarded dimensions. End on the open burden: recovering structure to beat the fair bar is not yet shown.

6. Credibility coda

One paragraph: you will only defend this claim under the peer-review checklist, including against the baseline you hope to lose to later.

Thesis bridge

Why this essay is the mission's hinge. The skeptic's strongest objection is still Grinsztajn's: "trees already win on tabular data — why bother with RDL?" The synthesis is how you absorb that objection instead of dodging it. You grant the flat-table result, explain it with the three biases, show that single-table upgrades have plateaued, and then point at the join as the unpaid axis — while admitting that Year 1 has demonstrated the cost of flattening, not yet a fair win from keeping structure. L040 will ask you to beat XGBoost on a flat task or explain why not; this essay is the explanation you will revise or stand on.

The lab for this lesson is the essay itself — not a notebook. Draft it from the skeleton above, citing evidence of record (lesson numbers and the verified figures). Then paste it into chat. Your teacher will grade it as a hostile competent reader: Does it argue rather than recap? Does it distinguish wins from ties? Does it name boundaries and the open burden?
