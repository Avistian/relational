TabNet (sequential attention)

Lesson 043 · Phase 2 · Q1 · primary reading: Arik & Pfister 2019, TabNet: Attentive Interpretable Tabular Learning

Last lesson you built the bar. L042 trained a tuned MLP and ResNet from scratch under one shared protocol and established the baseline-first rule: the strong simple baselines are run first, because a "win" over a weak or unequally-tuned net measures a tuning-effort gap, not model quality. This lesson is the first time we point that bar at a novel architecture — and the first time the answer is unflattering.

TabNet is the most-cited attempt at a "canonical" deep architecture for tables. Its idea is genuinely interesting and worth understanding on its own terms: instead of feeding every feature into every layer, it takes several decision steps, and at each step it chooses a small subset of features to look at — a learned, sparse, per-row feature selection. That gives it two claims: a useful inductive bias (capacity is not wasted on irrelevant columns) and interpretability for free (the selection masks tell you which features it used).

Its single skill: implement TabNet's sequential attention from scratch — sparsemax, the attentive transformer, and the prior scale — train it, read its masks as feature attributions, and hold it to the baseline-first rule against the tuned MLP/ResNet from L042.

One sentence. TabNet's masks really do recover which features matter when relevance is global (verified: 77% of the mask mass on the four truly-relevant columns of the paper's own Syn2), but on four small real tables it lands behind both simple baselines from L042 (mean rank 2.50 vs MLP 1.75 and ResNet 2.00) — so the honest verdict on the first novel architecture is interesting mechanism, unproven advantage.

Why sparse feature selection is the idea

Start from why a plain MLP is a poor fit for tables (L025–L027). A tree carves the space with axis-aligned splits on one feature at a time, and it ignores uninformative columns almost for free. An MLP mixes every feature into every unit from the first layer, so noise columns get multiplied in everywhere and the model must learn to undo them.

TabNet's bet (paper §"TabNet for Tabular Learning", Fig. 3) is that you can build a tree-like decision manifold out of ordinary neural blocks if you add one ingredient: a multiplicative mask that selects features before the block processes them. Mask, then transform, then add the pieces up — that is a soft, learned version of "split on this feature, then that one."

Decision step. One round of "choose features → process them → contribute to the answer." TabNet runs N_steps of these (the paper's sweet spot is 3–10), and sums their contributions into the final prediction. Loosely analogous to depth, but each step also decides what it is allowed to see — that is the part no MLP does.
Instance-wise (per-row) feature selection. The selected subset is computed from the row itself, so two rows can use different features. This is strictly more expressive than global selection (one fixed subset for the whole dataset, e.g. Lasso or a feature-importance cut). It matters whenever relevance is conditional — "for retail customers look at basket size, for wholesale look at contract terms."

The mechanism, piece by piece

Five components, each a box in the diagram above. We define each one fully, including the pieces you have not met yet.

1. sparsemax — the reason the mask can contain real zeros

A mask that "selects" features must be able to output exactly zero for a feature. Softmax cannot: exp(z)/Σexp(z) is strictly positive, so every feature always leaks through with some small weight. TabNet uses sparsemax (Martins & Astudillo 2016) instead.

sparsemax. The Euclidean projection of a vector onto the probability simplex: among all vectors that are non-negative and sum to 1, it returns the one closest to the input. Because the simplex has flat faces and corners, the closest point frequently lies on a face where some coordinates are exactly 0 — that is where the sparsity comes from. Concretely (Algorithm 1): sort z descending → z₍₁₎ ≥ z₍₂₎ ≥ … ≥ z₍K₎ k(z) = max{ k : k·z₍k₎ > (Σ_{j≤k} z₍j₎) − 1 } τ(z) = ( (Σ_{j≤k(z)} z₍j₎) − 1 ) / k(z) sparsemax(z)ᵢ = max(zᵢ − τ(z), 0) In words: find a single threshold τ, subtract it from every coordinate, and clip negatives to zero — with τ chosen precisely so the survivors sum to 1. Coordinates below the threshold are switched off, not merely made small.

Those four lines are much easier to believe as a picture. Draw the logits as bars and τ as a water line: sparsemax keeps exactly the parts that poke above it. Drag the spread and watch two things the algebra hides — the total shaded area never changes (that is precisely what solving for τ guarantees), and the number of survivors is decided by how separated the logits are, not by any threshold anyone sets.

One line of the paper, drawn. The two extremes are the ones to sit with. At flat, τ goes negative, every feature survives, and sparsemax has quietly become a dense mask — sparsity is a property of the logits, not a promise of the operator. At peaked, one feature's lead exceeds the mask's entire budget of 1 and everything else is crushed to exactly zero. Softmax, on the same logits at every setting, never reaches zero. The mask-entropy figure in the readout is the quantity L_sparse penalises (§4 below).

Two consequences worth holding onto. First, the mask still sums to 1 across features, so it behaves like an attention distribution — the model is allocating a fixed budget of attention, not scaling features freely. Second, gradients flow only through the surviving coordinates, so an unselected feature receives no gradient at that step.

2. The attentive transformer — how the mask is computed

At step i, the mask is produced from the previous step's leftover representation a[i−1]:

M[i] = sparsemax( P[i−1] · h_i(a[i−1]) )

where h_i is a single fully-connected layer followed by batch normalisation, and P[i−1] is the prior scale defined next. M[i] has one weight per input feature; the step then processes the masked row M[i] · f.

Where a[0] comes from. The formula reads the previous step's representation, which leaves step 1 with nothing to read. The paper's Fig. 4a answers this and the equations do not: before any masking happens, TabNet runs one extra feature transformer over the unmasked features and keeps the a half of its output as a[0] — the seed for the first mask. The d half of that step-0 output is discarded; it never reaches the prediction. It is the top row of the diagram above, and it is the piece people most often leave out when reimplementing TabNet from the equations alone.

3. The prior scale — what makes the attention sequential

If each step computed its mask independently, all steps would tend to grab the same few strong features. The prior scale is the memory that prevents this:

P[i] = ∏_{j≤i} ( γ − M[j] ) P[0] = 1
Prior scale P and the relaxation factor γ. P[i] records how much each feature has already been used. Every step multiplies its attention logits by it, so a feature that earlier steps spent gets discounted and sparsemax is more likely to zero it out. γ controls how strict this is: at γ = 1, a feature that received the full mask weight 1 has prior exactly 1 − 1 = 0 afterwards and is banned from every later step; as γ grows, reuse is permitted. The paper searches γ ∈ {1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 2.0} and notes a larger N_steps usually wants a larger γ.

4. The sparsity penalty — how sparse is "sparse"

sparsemax can produce zeros; a regulariser encourages it to. TabNet adds the mask's entropy to the loss:

L_sparse = Σ_i Σ_b Σ_j −M_{b,j}[i] · log( M_{b,j}[i] + ε ) / (N_steps · B)

Entropy is maximal when the mask is spread evenly and zero when it is one-hot, so minimising it pushes each step toward committing to few features. It enters the total loss with a coefficient λ_sparse. The paper's ablation (Table 11) shows both directions can hurt: too high (λ = 0.01) costs 3.1 accuracy points on Forest Cover, and turning it off entirely costs 0.5.

5. The feature transformer — the part that does the actual computing

GLU (gated linear unit). A fully-connected layer emits twice the values it needs, splits them in half, and returns a · sigmoid(b): half the activations act as a learned gate on the other half. It is the non-linearity TabNet uses instead of ReLU, and the paper's ablation shows the swap matters (replacing GLU with ReLU costs up to 3.1 points).
Shared vs step-dependent layers. Every step sees the same input features, so it would be wasteful to relearn low-level processing at each step. The paper's default splits the feature transformer into 2 layers shared across all steps plus 2 step-dependent layers — parameter-efficient, and the ablation shows this partial sharing beats both fully shared and fully independent.
The √0.5 residual. Blocks are wired as (x + block(x)) · √0.5. Adding two signals of similar variance roughly doubles the variance; multiplying by √0.5 restores it, so activations do not grow as blocks stack (Gehring et al. 2017). Same motive as L042's skip connections, with an explicit rescale.
Ghost BatchNorm and the virtual batch size B_V. TabNet trains with very large batches (the paper suggests 1–10% of the training set). Normalising such a large batch as one unit averages away too much of the noise that helps generalisation, so BatchNorm is applied independently to virtual sub-batches of size B_V (Hoffer et al. 2017). One detail that bites in practice: the paper's momentum m_B ∈ [0.6, 0.98] is TensorFlow-style decay, so a PyTorch implementation must pass 1 − m_B.

Those four definitions — the gate, the sharing, the rescaled residual, the ghost batch norm — are all one block, and it is far easier to hold as a picture than as a list. This is the paper's Fig. 4c, with an inset that opens a single layer:

Checked against FeatureTransformer.forward in labs/relkit/tabnet.py. Note the asymmetry the diagram makes obvious and the prose does not: the first layer is the only one without a residual, because it is the only one that changes the width (D → N_d + N_a) — there is nothing of matching size to add it to.

Each step splits its transformer output in two: d[i] (width N_d) contributes to the prediction, and a[i] (width N_a) is what the next step's attention reads. The contributions are aggregated tree-style and mapped to the output:

[ d[i], a[i] ] = f_i( M[i] · f ) d_out = Σ_i ReLU( d[i] ) ŷ = W_final · d_out

Reading the masks — on the paper's own data

"Interpretability" is a claim that must be tested, and you cannot test it on real data: you do not know which features truly matter. So the paper uses synthetic datasets where the answer is known by construction (Table 1, Fig. 5), and so do we. Two of them, from L2X (Chen et al. 2018) in the 11-feature form TabNet cites:

  • Syn2 — P(y=1) ∝ exp(X₃² + X₄² + X₅² + X₆² − 4). Only X₃–X₆ matter, and they matter for every row: relevance is global.
  • Syn4 — if X₁₁ < 0 the label comes from exp(X₁·X₂), otherwise from the Syn2 rule. Relevance is instance-wise: no fixed subset is right for all rows, and a model must read X₁₁ to know where to look.

The mask does react to the switch: X₁₁ itself gets weight 0.118 (the largest single feature), and the X₁–X₂ mass more than doubles (0.089 → 0.211) on exactly the rows where that group is the relevant one. But it is only a partial recovery — on 84% of those rows the mask still puts more weight on X₃–X₆ than on the group that actually generates the label.

Read that honestly — and note what the paper itself says. The direction is right and the switch feature is found, so the mechanism is doing something real. But "TabNet gives you instance-wise interpretability" is stronger than what a 6000-row run supports. The paper is candid about the cost of sharp masks: for its Fig. 5 visualisations it trained on 10M samples rather than 10k precisely because that is what made the selection masks sharp. So the honest lesson is: masks are evidence, not ground truth — their crispness is itself a function of how much data you had. The XOR group (X₁·X₂) is also the hardest case for a mask, because neither feature is informative alone; only the product is.

Friedman χ² = 5.70, p = 0.127 across the four datasets.

What p = 0.127 licenses you to say — and what it does not. The Friedman test across four datasets does not reach significance, so the disciplined statement is: we cannot distinguish these four models on this sample. That cuts both ways. You may not claim "TabNet is significantly worse" from this run — but neither may TabNet's advocate claim it is better, and the burden of proof sits with the new model. Under the baseline-first rule the outcome is unambiguous in the only sense that matters: TabNet did not clear the bar. It ranks behind both simple baselines it was supposed to beat, and its one clear-signal result is a loss. Compare L042, where the same harness on the same tables gave p = 0.039 — a reminder that adding a fourth model changes the test's sensitivity, and that a small N makes any single p fragile (L023).
And this is four datasets — a demonstration, not the verdict. The representative claim comes from the large benchmarks, and they agree with the direction found here: Gorishniy 2021 finds TabNet well behind a properly-tuned ResNet, and Grinsztajn 2022 (~45 datasets) does not place it near the top either. Our four tables reproduce the method and the direction; the strength of the claim is borrowed from the benchmarks, never from our N.

What we did about it, and what is still open. Our model scoring higher than the reference is the suspicious direction — it usually means the comparison is unfair rather than the model being good. So we tested two explanations and both were refuted: (1) training length — giving the reference patience 25 / 200 epochs left credit_g at 0.686, no better; (2) the learning-rate schedule — our loop implements the paper's "large initial rate, gradually decayed" (Appendix F) and the library's default fit() does not, but adding the same StepLR decay to the reference gave 0.680, also no better. So the credit_g discrepancy is unexplained, and the correct thing to write down is exactly that: mechanism validated exactly; end-to-end agreement holds on one table and fails on another, cause not yet identified. The most likely remaining suspect is that the reference runs its own training loop, so "identical protocol" never fully transfers — you can only hold a library to a shared protocol on the axes you actually control.
Why this does not rescue TabNet. Note the direction: the reference scores lower than our implementation (0.694 vs 0.748 on credit_g). Our from-scratch TabNet is the charitable version, and it still ranks behind the tuned MLP and ResNet. The lesson's conclusion is therefore robust to which implementation you trust.

So what is TabNet actually for?

Not nothing. Two things survive contact with the bar, and both matter later:

  • Sparse, learned, per-row feature selection as an inductive bias. The mechanism is the first serious answer to "how do you give a neural net the tree-like ability to ignore columns?" — and it works best exactly where the paper says: many redundant features, plenty of data. Its own Appendix A is refreshingly honest that on saturated KDD tables it merely ties XGBoost and CatBoost.
  • Attention as built-in attribution. Masks come out of the forward pass, so you get attributions without a separate SHAP/LIME step. That idea does not die with TabNet — it reappears in FT-Transformer (L046) and, crucially for us, in the relational models of Years 4–5, where attention over joined rows is how an RDL model shows which related records drove a prediction.

Thesis bridge

What today buys the thesis. Two things, one methodological and one architectural. Methodologically, this is the first time we ran the baseline-first rule on a famous model and got a negative — good practice for the moment the model on trial is ours. When an RDL result eventually looks good, the same protocol must be pointed at it, with the same willingness to write down "did not clear the bar." Architecturally, sequential attention over features is the single-table rehearsal for attention over relational neighbourhoods: RelGT and the Relational Transformer decide which related rows to attend to, and the honest reading of masks you practised today — evidence, not ground truth — is exactly the discipline needed when a relational model claims to show you "which customer history mattered."
