TabM — parameter-efficient ensembling

One shared backbone, several predictions: trace the savings, the objective, and the evidence.

L053 asked what a strong training recipe can buy a plain MLP. TabM asks a different question: can we obtain useful ensemble predictions while sharing expensive weights? Your outcome is to implement the numeric TabM-mini, trace its member axis from input to loss, and separate architecture, training procedure and benchmark evidence. For our relational mission it supplies a demanding single-table baseline: a relational model must add value beyond good flat features and a well-evaluated ensemble.

Read Gorishniy, Kotelnikov & Babenko, TabM: Advancing Tabular Deep Learning with Parameter-Efficient Ensembling (arXiv 2410.24210, ICLR 2025), §3 (the model) and §5 (the analysis), in one sitting; use the notebook for the implementation and evidence in a second. We build TabM-mini — the minimal effective variant — with numeric inputs only.

Recall before reading

1. The overlooked opportunity: ensembling

A deep ensemble averages predictions from networks trained with independent seeds. Its advantage is conditional: if members make complementary errors, averaging can help. It need not beat the best member, and duplicating one model changes nothing. Storing k independent networks costs approximately k times one network's parameters; training also repeats the optimization.

Parameter-efficient ensembling shares the large matrices while retaining small member-specific parameters and multiple output heads. TabM adopts BatchEnsemble and modifies its sharing and initialization. The saving concerns parameters first. Activations still have a member axis and dense multiply-adds still scale with k. Runtime overhead can be less than k on hardware that the original MLP underuses; this is a measurement, not a guarantee of nearly free inference. Paper §3.2 and Appendix A.1.

There are two separate experimental levers. Weight sharing constrains which functions the members can represent. Ensemble-aware selection chooses hyperparameters and the stopping epoch using the average prediction's validation metric. The paper's architecture ladder probes both; our four-arm experiment combines several differences and therefore cannot identify either as the unique cause of an improvement.

Why averaging helps only some kinds of error¶

Write member i's regression prediction as the true conditional mean plus an error eᵢ. The ensemble error is the average of those errors. If each member has error variance σ² and every pair has correlation ρ, the variance of the k-member average is σ²[ρ+(1−ρ)/k]. This follows by expanding the variance of a sum into k variance terms and k(k−1) covariance terms. The widget and fixed-correlation k→∞ discussion restrict ρ to [0,1]; negative equal pairwise correlations must satisfy ρ≥−1/(k−1) and cannot stay fixed negative as k grows.

For k=4 and ρ=0, the variance is σ²/4. For ρ=0.75 it is 0.8125σ². Adding members cannot average away the shared component. As k grows, the expression approaches ρσ², not zero. The calculation assumes equal variances and equal pairwise correlation; use it as intuition rather than a fitted law for every TabM ensemble.

Bias matters as well. If every member makes the same systematic mistake, averaging preserves it. “Diversity” is useful when different predictions contain complementary information about the target. Randomly making a strong predictor worse can increase disagreement without improving the ensemble.

TabM aims to obtain useful diversity with shared expensive matrices and relatively cheap member-specific parameters. This creates a tension: sharing reduces parameter storage and can couple learning beneficially, but it also prevents members from being independent fits. The experiment should measure the collective result, individual members and cost rather than assuming independence from the member count.

Three axes that a single ensemble score hides¶

Separate member quality, error dependence and aggregation. A weak member may help if its useful errors differ from the rest; a strong but redundant member may add little. The aggregation rule can also change the result even with identical members. Inspect these axes before interpreting why the ensemble improved or failed.

2. Model architecture

The pictured model is numeric TabM-mini, corrected v2, with shared training batches (the paper's §3.4 simplification). B is rows in a batch, p is numeric features, k is members, d is hidden width, N is the number of blocks and dy is output width (1 for regression, 2 for our binary tasks). Median imputation and z-score statistics are fitted only on training rows. Regression targets are standardized on training rows and errors converted back to their original units.

Broadcast x from [B,p] to [B,k,p]. Multiply by the learned input adapter R₁:[k,p], initialized to random ±1. Its entries are continuous trainable scalars after initialization, not permanently binary masks. Then repeat N blocks: a shared linear transformation with shared bias, ReLU max(0,h), and dropout. Dropout independently masks activations during training and is disabled at inference. Mini has no S adapter anywhere, no later R, and no member-specific backbone bias. The remaining outputs [B,k,d] pass through k independent heads [k,d,dy].

For classification, softmax each member's dy logits and average the probabilities over k. For regression average the scalar outputs. Labels reach a mean-of-member-losses objective during training; they are never input features. The independent heads, input adapters and training randomness allow member differences; the first adapter is not their only possible source.

Full TabM keeps input/output scaling vectors R,S and member biases in every block. All multiplicative adapters except R₁ start at 1; member bias rows start equal to an ordinary random linear bias. They can diverge while learning. The paper's † variants apply numerical feature embeddings before the backbone; Appendix A.2 uses a normal first-adapter initialization for those variants. Our plain-numeric scope excludes embeddings, one-hot categories and multiclass training. §3.3, Figure 1, A.2.

Count the saving instead of calling it free

For p=8, d=64, N=3, scalar output and k=32, the shared backbone has (8×64+64)+2(64×64+64)=8,896 parameters. One MLP adds 65 head parameters: 8,961. Corrected mini adds 32×8 input scales and 32×65 head parameters: 11,232 total. Independent MLP×32 stores 286,752 parameters. Mini is about 1.25 times one MLP and 3.9% of the independent ensemble's parameter count in this example. Its hidden activations still have shape[B,32,64], so this arithmetic establishes storage savings, not equivalent runtime or activation memory.

3. The BatchEnsemble layer

This is the load-bearing operation. Take any linear layer l(x) = Wx + b. A deep ensemble gives member i its own full matrix Wᵢ. BatchEnsemble instead keeps one shared W and gives member i three small adapters: two multiplicative vectors rᵢ (applied to the input) and sᵢ (applied to the output), and an additive bias bᵢ. The member computes (§3.2):

lᵢ(x) = sᵢ ⊙ ( W ( rᵢ ⊙ x ) ) + bᵢ   which equals giving member i the matrix   Wᵢ = W ⊙ ( sᵢ rᵢᵀ ).

Here ⊙ is coordinate-wise multiplication and sᵢ rᵢᵀ is the outer product (a rank-one matrix whose (o,i) entry is sᵢ[o]·rᵢ[i]). So each member reuses the shared W but rescales its rows and columns by learned scalar factors (±1 only at initialization). Adding a member costs only 3d new numbers per layer — small beside W's d² when d is large. Original BatchEnsemble initializes its multiplicative adapters randomly with ±1. Full TabM instead initializes only the first R randomly; mini retains only that R. Stacked over members, the layer runs all k in parallel: l_BE(X) = ((X ⊙ R) W) ⊙ S + B with X of shape [k, d]. Source: §3.2.

Toggle the ±1 signs below. At initialisation TabM sets the non-first adapters to 1 (not random), so Wᵢ = W exactly — the adapter has no effect yet — and the model behaves like TabM-mini; training then frees those adapters to add expressivity. Watch that the two computation routes always agree: the adapter route and the explicit Wᵢ·x are the same layer.

Two matrix conventions, one operation

The paper's single-object equation uses column vectors and W:[dout,din], hence its mask is srᵀ. The notebook stores the transpose W:[din,dout] and multiplies row vectors on the right, hence its mask is rsᵀ. For rectangular W, these shapes expose a mistaken transpose immediately. For width d, 3d added parameters per member describes a full BatchEnsemble layer, not mini's single input adapter.

The multiplier rsᵀ has rank one, but W⊙(rsᵀ)=diag(r)Wdiag(s) generally has the same rank as W when the scaling entries are nonzero. This is multiplicative row/column scaling, unlike adding a low-rank LoRA update. It cannot freely move one matrix entry without coupling an entire row or column.

Follow two members through one shared matrix¶

Use row-vector convention. Let x=(2,3), shared W=[[1,2],[3,4]], zero bias and output adapters S=(1,1). Member A has input adapter Rₐ=(1,1); member B has Rᵦ=(1,−1). A sends (2,3) into W and obtains (11,16). B sends (2,−3) into the same W and obtains (−7,−8). The matrix is shared; the effective function differs.

In this convention, member i's effective matrix entry is Rᵢ,a Wₐ,b Sᵢ,b. The adapter multiplies an entire input direction and an entire output direction. It cannot independently choose every entry of a dense matrix. Calling the adapter a low-rank multiplicative modulation does not mean that the resulting effective matrix must itself have low rank.

For width d on both sides, the shared matrix uses d² parameters. A full BatchEnsemble layer adds roughly k(2d+d) parameters when both multiplicative adapters and member biases are included. Independent dense layers instead use about k(d²+d). At large d, sharing the d² term can save substantial storage. Activations still carry a member axis, so compute and activation memory do not become independent of k.

Corrected v2 mini keeps only the first input adapter R, ordinary shared backbone biases, and independent output heads. It has no S adapter or member-specific backbone biases. The preserved v1 operator included those extra parameters and is historical evidence, not this architecture. The full local TabM variant uses adapters through the backbone. These variants must not be conflated in an architecture caption: their sharing patterns are different.

The first random sign adapter breaks member symmetry. Later adapters initialized to one initially preserve the shared transformation. If every member had identical parameters, identical inputs and identical stochastic behavior, gradient updates would have no reason to separate them. The initialization is therefore a load-bearing part of the ensemble construction.

4. Why weight sharing and joint training

Follow the paper's controlled ladder (§3.3). MLP×k tunes one MLP, fits independent seeds, and selects each member at its own validation optimum. TabM-packed stores the same independent backbone architecture, but trains in parallel and selects/tunes the collective predictor. It is not the same experimental baseline. TabM-naive replaces independent matrices with BatchEnsemble sharing and random multiplicative adapters. TabM-mini removes all but R₁. TabM restores the adapters with the improved mostly-ones initialization. Figure 2 reports separately tuned variants, so a plotted gap is evidence about these full procedures.

Collective checkpointing can matter even with no weight sharing. Suppose member A's best validation epoch is 12 and B's is 30, but their averaged predictions are best at epoch 20. Independent checkpointing assembles A₁₂+B₃₀; collective checkpointing retains the two members at epoch 20. Their error dependence can differ. Shared parameters add another coupling: gradients from all members update the same W. Calling the branches independently supervised does not make their parameter updates independent.

The training objective is L=(1/Bk)Σb,i ℓ(zbi,yb) for shared batches. Cross-entropy is minus the log probability assigned to the true class; regression uses squared error. Inference averages member probabilities or scalar predictions. The notebook computes these as four live functions. Do not pool hidden representations and then apply one head: that destroys the separate prediction branches emphasized in Appendix A.4.

In the original independent-batch procedure, each member sees B independently shuffled rows, giving inputs [B,k,p] and targets [B,k]. Shared batches broadcast one batch's B rows to all k members. Both still average Bk losses, but they expose different training examples. Our small suite uses shared batches; the post-EXIT trainer exposes the independent permutations, including the correct matching target layout. §3.4.

The paper heuristically fixes k=32 rather than tuning it in the main benchmark. R,S and biases are learned parameters; width, dropout, learning rate and k are hyperparameters chosen outside gradient descent. Appendix A.3 hypothesizes a connection between TabM's often larger selected learning rate and its effective batch; this is not a proven causal explanation. Our small comparison intentionally fixes one learning rate across neural arms.

Average losses during training; average predictions at inference¶

For classification, the local objective computes each member's cross-entropy and then averages across members and rows. This asks every member to predict the target. It differs from first averaging member probabilities and then computing one cross-entropy for the ensemble.

Take a positive example with member probabilities 0.9 and 0.1. The mean member loss is [−ln(0.9)−ln(0.1)]/2≈1.204. The mean probability is 0.5, whose ensemble loss is −ln(0.5)≈0.693. The second objective can tolerate one member's confident failure when another compensates; the first directly penalizes both members' individual errors.

At inference, classification probabilities are averaged. Averaging logits and applying softmax afterward is generally a different operation. In binary form, probabilities 0.9 and 0.6 average to 0.75. Their logits are about 2.197 and 0.405; sigmoid of the average logit is about 0.786. Read the actual aggregation axis and the position of softmax.

For regression with squared error, averaging member predictions is natural, but the training distinction still exists. The mean squared member loss equals the squared loss of the mean prediction plus the variance of member predictions around that mean. This identity follows by expanding each deviation around the ensemble mean and canceling the cross terms.

Shared weights receive gradient contributions from all members. The update is a joint compromise; it is not equivalent to training k independent networks and compressing them afterward. This can act as a useful constraint, but it also means that pruning members after training changes an ensemble that was optimized jointly.

5. Weak individually, stronger collectively?

Before looking at scores, define the comparison. A standalone MLP is optimized with its own weights; a submodel is one branch of a jointly trained TabM. The mean individual error evaluates every branch separately then averages errors. The collective error first averages predictions then evaluates. These operations need not agree.

For squared error on one row, let μ=(1/k)Σpᵢ. Expanding pᵢ−y=(pᵢ−μ)+(μ−y) and canceling Σ(pᵢ−μ) gives meanᵢ(pᵢ−y)²=(μ−y)²+meanᵢ(pᵢ−μ)². Thus ensemble MSE cannot exceed mean member MSE. The analogous RMSE inequality follows from the triangle inequality for error vectors. For cross-entropy, convexity of −log gives the same direction when probabilities are averaged. These are arithmetic facts, so their presence alone is not evidence that TabM learned a better predictor than a separate MLP.

Classification error has no such guarantee. On one positive row, probabilities (0.51,0.51,0.01) give mean individual error 1/3 but mean probability 0.343 and collective error 1. The two slightly correct members are outweighed by one confident wrong member. A CHECK must verify the calculation and record the observed ordering, not reject a legitimate result because a preferred story failed.

Figure 5 is stronger evidence than the elementary inequality: the authors compare train/test trajectories against a matched width-512, depth-3, k=1 baseline, with dropout/weight decay off, 25 separately tried learning rates and training beyond the best epoch. Our checkpoint-only small suite does not reproduce those dynamics. Figure 6 evaluates the validation-selected best submodel across the benchmark. Our saved individual_best is an oracle minimum on test used only diagnostically; it is not a deployable member selection rule. §5.1, D.5.

The widget isolates equal-variance, equal-correlation errors under a zero-mean assumption. It changes correlation while holding member error scale fixed; it does not train TabM or estimate correlation from our data.

What counts as a dead neuron?

Section 5.4 measures whether a shared ReLU neuron ever activates over a fixed probe of 2,048 training objects and all member paths at the best validation checkpoint. Across 46 datasets the reported fractions are 0.29±0.17 for k=1 and 0.14±0.09 for k=32; these ± values describe variation across datasets, not uncertainty of one fixed neuron's state. More member paths offer more activation opportunities, consistent with the authors' utilization explanation. The statistic alone does not prove that fewer inactive units caused better generalization; we have not rerun this experiment. §5.4.

6. The k knob and submodel pruning

The paper's Figure 7 averages performance over 17 datasets, five seeds per score, dropout 0.1 and a learning rate retuned for each k. Larger backbones tended to accommodate more members; too high k sometimes hurt, and width 64 or depth 1 was suboptimal in those studied middle-to-large regimes. This does not prove every small TabM must fail or license comparing fixed-learning-rate curves as if they replicated Figure 7. §5.3, D.7.

Pruning is a different experiment. Appendix D.6 greedily adds the next member that most improves the current aggregate on validation, beginning with the best validation member. It stops when no new member helps and the current aggregate is already better than the original all-k validation result. Sorting members by standalone accuracy is not this algorithm. Section 5.2 reports 8.8±6.6 retained out of 32 across 46 datasets, with slightly worse test performance. Prune adapter rows and heads to save work; merely averaging fewer already-computed outputs does not establish a speedup. We explain this published experiment but do not reproduce it here. D.6.

Increasing k changes both the estimator and the computation¶

There are two different experiments. Train separate models at k=1,4,16 and compare their final predictors; or train once at k=16 and evaluate subsets of its members. The first changes the training objective's coupled system and computational budget. The second studies the redundancy of a fixed trained ensemble. A pruning curve from the second is not a training-scaling curve from the first.

If you select a subset using validation performance, subset choice is another adaptive decision. Choosing the best subset on test labels would leak evaluation feedback. A fixed first-m-member rule is easy to reproduce but can be sensitive to member ordering; randomized subsets with saved seeds provide a clearer view of average subset behavior.

Measure time on the intended device. A larger batched member dimension can use hardware more efficiently even while doing more arithmetic. Parameter counts alone cannot predict latency, and latency alone cannot describe peak activation memory. Report batch size, member count, precision and whether timing includes preprocessing.

Read weak members without dismissing the ensemble¶

Compare each member's loss with the aggregate loss and inspect paired error dependence. If members are individually weaker than standalone MLPs but their average is competitive, that is compatible with the method's purpose. It does not establish that weakness caused diversity or that any weak model would help.

A useful follow-up fixes the trained outputs and changes only aggregation or subset size. Another retrains under a fixed total compute budget. Keep these questions separate in the exit report, then state which evidence supports a practical choice of k.

7. What exactly are we comparing?

The corrected v2 suite compares a single MLP, independently checkpointed MLP×32, numeric TabM-mini with k=32, and validation-tuned XGBoost. California, House 16H and Higgs Small arrays come from the released TabR archive. Appendix C explicitly states that TabM inherits 38 TabR datasets, including these three; this is stronger provenance than simply matching dataset names. We retain those boundaries but cap train/validation/test at 1200/600/600 with label-blind selection seeds 53/54/55. We have not independently byte-compared a second TabM archive.

Model seeds are 0/1/2. Neural arms use train-only median/z-score transforms, width 64, depth 3, dropout .1, Adam .002, 64 epochs, batch 256 with the incomplete final batch dropped, and the first strictly best validation checkpoint. The deep ensemble selects each member independently. TabM uses shared batches and collective validation. XGBoost searches six depth/rate candidates, with 150-tree ceilings and validation stopping. These are equal data/metric boundaries, not equal tuning or compute budgets.

The small suite deliberately preserves the earlier budget to evaluate the corrected model without adding more training. It also fixes the checkpoint tie policy from last-best to first-best, which can change classifier scores; the v1/v2 difference is not a controlled estimate of initialization alone. It omits the paper's AdamW/clip1/patience16 procedure, usual modified quantile preprocessing, separately tuned neural recipes, feature embeddings, mostly 15 evaluation seeds, and 43 other datasets. The post-EXIT trainer adds independent member batches, normal quantiles, AdamW, gradient clipping and patience stopping in a separate track. Neither is a benchmark reproduction.

We save source hashes, exact selected row indices, raw array hashes, versions, predictions, plus single-MLP/TabM validation histories and selected epochs for v2. The independent deep ensemble saves aggregate predictions but does not retain every member checkpoint. The original v1 operators/results remain immutable historical evidence; their extra S₁, member biases and incorrect fan-in make those scores not measurements of the corrected mini architecture. Use each result with its own operator.

10. What the paper establishes, and what comes next

Figure 3 reports strong TabM-family tabular-DL performance on 46 tasks: 37 random splits and 9 domain-aware splits. Its relative-performance panels use accuracy/AUROC or R² relative to MLP; they are not mean raw RMSE. Appendix D.3 ranks models in tiers using means and seed SDs, whereas our local chart uses ordinary ranks. Separate the paper's numeric, † embedding and ensemble variants when comparing a particular score. §4.2.

Figure 4 times selected configurations. Asterisk-marked TabM variants additionally use mixed precision and torch.compile; the authors explicitly caution against a direct unqualified efficiency comparison with unoptimized baselines. Table 2 evaluates full Maps Routing (6.5M rows) and Weather (13M), reusing configurations selected on smaller training sets with the same validation/test boundaries; most models use three seeds, FT-Transformer one. TabR runs out of memory in that setup. This is measured applicability on those datasets/hardware, not a theorem that retrieval can never scale. §4.3, D.4.

A concrete future target: Appendix E, Table 18 reports California numeric TabM-mini at RMSE 0.4479 ± 0.0022 for a single model (which itself contains k=32 branches). Its separate ensemble column averages five independently trained whole models and reports 0.4461 ± 0.0011. The † mini uses feature embeddings and reports 0.4275 ± 0.0024; it is a different target. These are cited seed mean/SDs under the paper procedure, not a tolerance for declaring our capped fixed recipe reproduced. Table 18.

Connect the mechanism to the nearest alternatives

RealMLP (L053) changes the parameterization, preprocessing and optimization recipe of a single predictor. TabM changes how several prediction branches share parameters; the ideas are potentially complementary, but combining them is a new experiment. FT-Transformer (L050) lets columns exchange information through attention within each row. TabM's dense W mixes feature coordinates while its member axis remains separate; attention across members would alter its intended objective and branches.

TabR (L052) conditions a row on retrieved training examples and their labels at prediction time. TabM conditions on a fixed fitted set of weights, with no runtime label memory. For a customer-churn table, all TabM branches still see the same engineered history columns; more members cannot reveal the customer's unjoined service events. This explains why a strong TabM baseline matters to the relational thesis without answering it. An eventual relational comparison must give both models legal information under the same prediction cutoff.

For L055, retain the ensemble while changing the evaluation question: does its ranking survive when training customers precede test customers in time, with labels available only after a delay? A random-split ensemble win and a temporal-split deployment win need separate evidence. Ask the teacher about any derivation, code path or result you cannot defend.
