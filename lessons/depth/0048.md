### Derive an interaction from a prediction question

Suppose a score uses two features: visits x and average purchase value z. In a linear model, `s = a*x + b*z + c`, increasing visits by one changes the score by a, regardless of purchase value. That is the additive assumption. Adding `w*x*z` changes the visit effect to `a + w*z`: a visit can matter more for a customer whose purchases are valuable. This dependence of one feature's effect on another is the interaction we want the model to represent.

A neural network with nonlinear hidden layers can already approximate interactions. DCNv2 is useful because it gives multiplicative structure a direct parameterized route. The question is about how the representation and optimization encourage useful interactions under a finite budget, rather than whether one model class is mathematically capable of multiplication in principle. An explicit product can also be a poor inductive preference when the relevant relationship is mostly additive or the observed product is dominated by outliers.

The input to the cross network is x₀, a vector that combines normalized numerical coordinates and learned categorical embedding coordinates. A product of two embedding coordinates is not necessarily a human-readable conjunction such as “country is Poland AND plan is premium.” The representation is learned, so the cross network operates on a coordinate system that changes during training. Explain a cross in terms of those coordinates before giving it a business interpretation.

Scaling matters immediately. If one normalized coordinate lies near 1 and another raw coordinate lies near 100,000, their product and the corresponding gradients can have very different magnitudes from the intended initialization regime. Fitting preprocessing on training data controls the numerical problem without making it statistically harmless to use validation or test values. The vocabulary, imputation values, and scaling parameters are all fitted state; they travel with the final model.

### Why retain the original vector?

The cross stack keeps two vectors with different roles. x₀ is the original embedded row, used as an anchor at every layer. xₗ is the current representation, which already contains earlier interactions. Multiplying a learned mixture of xₗ by x₀ extends those interactions while retaining a consistent connection to the original coordinates. Overwriting x₀ with xₗ changes the recurrence. It might still train, but it would no longer be the operator whose degree and factorization we analyze here. [Dense cross definition and architecture](https://arxiv.org/html/2008.13535v2#S3).

### What changed from the original DCN?

Set every row of W to the same vector wᵀ and temporarily set all biases to zero. Then every coordinate receives the same scalar mixture `t = wᵀxₗ`, and the update is `x₀*t`. This is the original DCN's multiplicative restriction. V2 gives each output coordinate its own row of W, hence its own mixture. The comparison concerns the cross operation; arbitrary bias conventions and final neural networks require separate comparisons. [Paper §3.2 and §4.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2008.13535v2#S4.SS2).

For x₀=xₗ=[2,3] and w=[1,−1], the shared mixture is −1. Both original coordinates are multiplied by −1, giving update [−2,−3] and residual output [0,0]. Instead choose V2 matrix `[[1,−1],[0,1]]`. Its mixtures are [−1,3], so its update is [−2,9] and output [0,12]. No shared scalar can produce both update ratios −1 and 3 for this nonzero anchor. This exhibits extra freedom at the vector-valued layer; it is not a proof that every trained V2 classifier outperforms every original DCN classifier.

### Read coefficients after the prediction head

For a one-layer cross-only score, let q be the linear readout vector and c its intercept. Expanding `s=qᵀ[x+x⊙(Wx+b)]+c` gives a linear term `Σᵢ qᵢ(1+bᵢ)xᵢ` and a quadratic term `Σᵢⱼ qᵢWᵢⱼxᵢxⱼ`. The coefficient of a square xᵢ² is qᵢWᵢᵢ. For distinct coordinates i and j, the coefficient of xᵢxⱼ is **qᵢWᵢⱼ+qⱼWⱼᵢ**, because multiplication commutes. A single matrix entry is therefore not the whole prediction-level interaction coefficient.

With q=[1,1], a skew-symmetric change `A=[[0,a],[−a,0]]` changes the vector representation but contributes `a*x₁*x₂−a*x₂*x₁=0` to its summed score. This is a precise example of non-unique weights for the same function. It prevents treating a large entry or block norm of W as causal feature importance. At greater depth, coefficients also collect products along multiple layer paths. The paper's §7.6 weight-block visualization is an inspection aid; it does not identify causal effects. A nonlinear deep head or learned embeddings further complicate interpretation.

### Expand a two-layer example all the way to the output

Use one scalar input x, zero biases, and cross weights 0.5 and 0.25. The first layer gives `x₁ = x + 0.5*x²`. The second layer still multiplies by the original x: `x₂ = x₁ + 0.25*x*x₁`. Substituting the first expression gives `x₂ = x + 0.75*x² + 0.125*x³`. This is a polynomial containing first-, second-, and third-degree terms. At x=2, the first layer gives 4 and the second gives `4 + 0.25×2×4 = 6`; the expanded polynomial gives the same 6.

The argument generalizes by induction. “By induction” means establish a starting case, then show that one additional layer preserves the proposed rule. Before any cross layer, each coordinate has degree one in x₀. A linear mixture of coordinates does not increase the maximum degree. Multiplying that mixture by one coordinate of x₀ increases its degree by at most one. Adding the residual preserves the lower-degree terms. After L linear cross layers, the maximum degree is therefore at most L+1 in the embedded input coordinates.

“At most” is essential. Weights can be zero, terms can cancel, or the readout can ignore a coordinate. Depth makes higher-degree terms available; it does not guarantee that the fitted model uses every degree. Nor does it make all coefficients independently adjustable. The shared matrices and repeated recurrence impose structure on which polynomial coefficients can be represented together. A count of possible monomials is not a count of free model parameters.

The bias inside the cross multiplication adds a term proportional to x₀. Moving it outside would add a different constant term at that layer. For an original coordinate x₀,j=0, the multiplicative update at that coordinate is zero, including its inside bias, so its cross-stack residual is preserved. The later prediction head can still depend on other coordinates and have its own bias. This is a useful coordinate-level CHECK because it distinguishes two plausible-looking formulas with identical tensor shapes.

### Follow the gradient through the residual route

Hold x₀ fixed temporarily and ask how a change in xₗ affects xₗ₊₁. There is a direct identity route through the residual addition and another route through the learned matrix followed by coordinate-wise scaling by x₀. In matrix notation, the derivative is `I + diag(x₀)Wₗ`. The diagonal matrix `diag(x₀)` places the anchor coordinates on its diagonal, so left-multiplying W scales its output rows.

This calculation explains the residual connection without promising easy optimization. The learned route can amplify, cancel, or distort the identity route. If you instead differentiate the complete network with respect to the original input, x₀ is not fixed: gradients also flow through its repeated appearances and through the embedding network. State which variable is being varied before using a derivative as an argument about stability.

### A rank constraint is a shared bottleneck

A dense d-by-d matrix can mix every current coordinate into every output coordinate. The factorization `W = U Vᵀ`, with U and V of shape `[d,r]`, first compresses xₗ to r intermediate coordinates, then expands those coordinates back to d. Here r is the rank budget. All output mixtures must be assembled through the same r-dimensional bottleneck, which restricts the possible matrix while reducing its stored parameters when r is small enough.

Take `V=[1,2]ᵀ`, `U=[1,3]ᵀ`, and current row `[2,3]`. The compressed scalar is `2×1 + 3×2 = 8`. Expanding it gives `[8,24]`. The equivalent dense matrix is `[[1,2],[3,6]]`; applying it to the column form of `[2,3]` gives exactly the same vector. With anchor `[2,3]` and zero bias, the coordinate-wise product is `[16,72]`, and adding the current row gives `[18,75]`.

| Computation | Intermediate | Shape for a batch |
|---|---|---|
| Compress with V | Weighted directions from current representation | `[B,r]` |
| Expand with U | One proposed update per original coordinate | `[B,d]` |
| Multiply by x₀ | Anchor-dependent interaction update | `[B,d]` |
| Add xₗ | Retain the residual representation | `[B,d]` |

The two-dimensional example establishes equivalence, not savings: its two factors contain four numbers, just like the dense matrix. At d=128 and r=16, the dense weights contain 16,384 numbers while the two factors contain 4,096, excluding the same bias vector. The parameter reduction is fourfold. Actual runtime depends on the two matrix multiplications, batch shape, memory traffic, and hardware; a smaller parameter count does not guarantee the same factor of speedup.

A copied-weight equivalence test requires constructing W from the very same U and V. Comparing a separately trained dense network with a separately trained factored network is not an algebraic equivalence test, even if their scores are similar. Conversely, exact equality for a factorable W does not show that low rank can express every dense W or that either parameterization finds the same solution during optimization.

### Parallel and stacked are different hypotheses about useful representations

In the parallel model, the cross branch and the ordinary MLP both receive x₀. If the final cross vector has width d and the final deep vector has width h, concatenation gives width d+h. The head can use explicit cross features, deep features, or a combination. This adds capacity as well as a second representation route, so a fair ablation should say which dimensions and parameter counts changed when a branch was added.

In the stacked model, the MLP receives x_L, the final cross representation. Its input already contains the structured multiplicative features. The MLP can transform them nonlinearly, but there is no separate direct deep branch reading x₀ unless the implementation explicitly adds one. Merely reusing the same MLP class does not make the two models equivalent: the arrays supplied to that class differ.

Consider a wiring test with a deep function that doubles its input. If x₀ is `[1,2]` and x_L is `[3,4]`, the parallel head receives `[3,4,2,4]`, while the stacked head receives `[6,8]`. These values make the architectural distinction visible without training a model. A shape check alone would miss a bug if two alternate routes happened to have the same width.

### Mixtures require a second layer of reasoning

A mixture of cross experts computes multiple proposed updates and combines them with input-dependent gate weights. With expert updates `[1,2]` and `[-1,4]` and weights `[0.75,0.25]`, the mixture update is `[0.5,2.5]`. Adding current state `[2,3]` produces `[2.5,5.5]`. The weights sum to one, but the resulting coordinates need not lie between zero and one: the gate is a probability-like allocation over experts, not a class probability.

If gate weights depend on the input, changing the input changes both each expert's proposal and the choice between proposals. Nonlinearities inside the experts introduce further non-polynomial operations. The simple degree-at-most-L+1 argument for linear cross layers therefore cannot be transferred to the nonlinear gated mixture. A useful comparison isolates dense versus factored linear crosses before adding nonlinear experts and routing, because the latter changes several assumptions at once.

### Determine what the comparison could actually answer

The three local tables provide a bounded check of the implemented training procedure. They are not automatically representative of the web-scale recommendation regime that motivated the paper. Their category counts, sparsity, target definitions, sample sizes, and validation procedures affect whether explicit crosses are useful. If the cross model loses, distinguish “this recipe lost here” from “multiplicative features cannot help.” If it wins, distinguish the measured gain from a guarantee about a future relational or ranking task.

The MovieLens task makes another distinction concrete. Predicting a raw rating with squared error and predicting whether a selected rating belongs to a binary class are different tasks. ## DCNv2: derive, implement, diagnose

`xₗ₊₁ = xₗ + x₀ ⊙ (Wₗxₗ + bₗ)` uses the original embedded vector x₀ at every layer. The bias is inside the elementwise product. With row-major batches, PyTorch's stored `[out,in]` matrix requires the corresponding transpose in explicit multiplication.

| Variant | Computation | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Dense cross | Full W | Flexible coordinate mixtures |
| Linear factorization | `W=UVᵀ` | r shared intermediate directions |
| Parallel | concatenate crossed state and MLP(x₀) | Two direct representation branches |
| Stacked | MLP(final crossed state) | Deep branch receives interactions |
| Nonlinear mixture | Input-dependent gated expert updates | Linear-cross polynomial argument no longer applies |

For zero-bias scalar weights .5 and .25, two layers yield `x + .75x² + .125x³`; at x=2 the result is 6. For d-dimensional linear crosses, depth L gives degree at most L+1 in embedded coordinates, not independently adjustable arbitrary coefficients.

Use a coordinate-zero fixture to check bias placement, named-coordinate values to check matrix orientation, constructed `W=UVᵀ` to check factor equivalence, and deliberately different x₀/x_L arrays to check branch wiring. Treat later training and paper-score agreement as separate tests.

Original DCN's zero-bias multiplicative restriction is W=1wᵀ: all output coordinates share one scalar mixture. V2 permits different rows of W. For a one-layer cross-only score with linear readout q, the coefficient of xᵢxⱼ (i≠j) is qᵢWᵢⱼ+qⱼWⱼᵢ. Reading W alone as causal feature importance is unjustified.
