A leaderboard is a conditional claim

TabArena: read the protocol, reconstruct the summary, bound the conclusion.

Open the guided lab · Download notebook · Reference

Your tangible win is a one-page audit of a published leaderboard. You will do three things with it. First, identify the evaluation population and the fitted procedure behind each score. Second, reconstruct the ranks yourself from the individual results. Third, say which deployment claim the evidence actually supports. Doing this raises the single-table baseline that your relational thesis must beat.

In plain terms. A leaderboard looks like a verdict about which model is "best." It is really a verdict about which model won one specific contest, on one specific set of tables, under one specific set of rules. This lesson teaches you to read those rules, so you know exactly what a ranking does and does not promise.

Route. Cold retrieval → protocol and worked trace → notebook audit → EXIT. Allow about 30–40 minutes for the core reading, and 35–50 minutes for the lab; the cached-score computation takes seconds. No new model is introduced. The load-bearing implementation you build is the evaluator.

A random split estimates performance on a similar draw from the pooled population; a temporal split evaluates later observations after earlier training. Seeds vary fitting randomness under fixed data, not the future population. Selection uses training/validation information; the outer test estimates the performance of the selected procedure.

A leaderboard is the end of a reduction, not the beginning

Temporal evaluation made the test distribution explicit. TabArena adds another layer: the ranking depends on which fitted procedures, tasks and splits are counted and how their errors are reduced. Follow one score through that reduction. The benchmark then becomes an auditable experiment rather than a list of model names.

2. Freeze the question before the ranking

In plain terms. Before you look at who won, you have to pin down what the contest actually was. Which tables were played on? What counts as a "method"? What rule decided the winner? If you skip this step, you can read a true ranking and still draw a false conclusion about your own problem.

Primary reading. Erickson et al., TabArena, version 1, §§2–3. Read §2.3 and Figure 1 first; then §2.2’s dataset criteria.

Start with one term. A living benchmark is a benchmark that is maintained over time. Its datasets, its model implementations, and its posted results can all change from one month to the next. So a reproducible statement about it must identify the exact snapshot you read, not just the website name.

What this version covers. The paper’s initial TabArena-v0.1 contains 51 manually curated datasets and 16 model families. It targets small-to-medium IID classification and regression. IID (independent and identically distributed) means the observations are treated as independent draws from one shared distribution, so random splitting is appropriate for the original task. The initial scope deliberately excludes three things: temporal or grouped dependencies, very small training sets below 500 rows, and large training sets above 250,000 rows. These are boundaries of this version. They are not permanent claims about every later TabArena release. Paper §2.2.

That is why L055 and L056 agree. A carefully curated IID benchmark can be excellent evidence for IID prediction, while leaving next-quarter prediction unanswered. An aggregate score cannot repair a mismatch in what the test population represents.

A leaderboard is a conditional statement¶

Everything a summary conditions on. A benchmark summary conditions on its dataset collection, its target definitions, its split generator, its model roster, its tuning resources, its ensembling rules, and its metric aggregation. It is evidence about that one constructed experiment. The presence of many model names in the leaderboard does not make it evidence about every future table.

What counts as one task. Begin with the unit of a task. Two targets derived from the same source table may share observations and feature structure. Several folds of one target are just repeated evaluations of that one task. Counting every result row as an independent dataset can make a large experiment look like it holds more independent information than it really does.

Inclusion decisions shape the population. The benchmark population also reflects which tasks were let in. Excluding extremely wide datasets, unsupported targets, or tasks with failed runs changes the population on which a mean is defined. Those choices may be necessary. But their consequences belong in the interpretation. “Best on completed tasks” and “best under a policy that includes failures” can point to different decisions.

Keep a denominator ledger. A useful audit starts by counting, at each stage: requested tasks, supported tasks, attempted fits, successful fits, and rows included in each aggregate. A model that is missing the difficult tasks should not receive an apparently comparable mean over an easier subset, unless the missingness policy is stated explicitly.

Scope check. For the local exercise, the archived metric errors are a finite evidence object. Recomputing them teaches aggregation and uncertainty. It does not launch the entire official search, and it does not create independent new model fits. Keep this distinction visible when you describe what your notebook has reproduced.

Curation is part of the statistical design¶

Four deduplication stages. Appendix B.1 describes four: normalize names; identify different names for the same data; remove alternate versions or targets; and remove closely related tasks from one source. Why go beyond filenames? Worked example. Suppose ten variants of one hospital dataset enter a 50-task benchmark. Then that single hospital can receive a fifth of the votes. A bootstrap cannot undo this: it only resamples the units it is given. Equal dataset weights are useful only after you have decided what deserves a distinct dataset identity.

IID is a property of the original task. The IID requirement refers to the original predictive task. A table of customer transactions may look like ordinary rows, yet still require grouping by customer. A sales table may require predicting a later period. Randomly partitioning either table changes the question being asked. TabArena's exclusion of such tasks is a scope decision, not a demonstration that such tasks are unimportant. L055 studies a different deployment target. For the relational mission, carry both lessons forward: choose a strong tabular procedure using IID evidence, then evaluate it and the relational alternative under the same appropriate entity/time split.

Curation is fallible. The curation is also contestable. Appendix B.3 reports inconsistent targets and preprocessing under the same dataset name, and describes why the authors read each dataset's provenance. Their checklist and public notes make these judgments reviewable. Manual curation is not proof that the 51 tasks randomly represent all business problems. When writing a conclusion, name the empirical population first; justify any wider inference separately.

Diagnostic question: all four methods lose every row of one difficult dataset before your coverage check. A pivot still looks perfectly rectangular. Can it detect the deletion? No: common coverage detects disagreement among methods; a separate expected-key manifest is needed to detect common omissions. Our hash-checked artifacts and asserted 51-dataset/816-split counts protect this frozen exercise. A newly submitted benchmark needs an external planned-task manifest, not just matching completed runs.

3. Name the fitted procedure

The comparison question specifies what evidence we want. Now identify the objects being compared: a default, tuned model and ensemble spend information and computation differently, even if they share a base-model name.

In plain terms. "CatBoost won" sounds like one model beat the others. But the thing that competed was a whole recipe: how the settings were chosen, how many models were bagged together, and how their predictions were combined. Two recipes can share the name "CatBoost" and still be different competitors. Naming the exact recipe is what lets you compare fairly.

A method is more than its model family. Three separate ideas hide inside a single family name. Hyperparameters are the choices that fix a training recipe, such as network width or learning rate. Learned weights are the numbers fitted from the training data once the recipe is fixed. HPO (hyperparameter optimization) uses validation performance to choose among recipes. A post-hoc ensemble combines the predictions of several already-trained configurations, using weights selected on validation predictions.

What the paper actually runs per family. The paper evaluates a default configuration plus 200 sampled configurations for tunable families, with exceptions such as TabICL and TabDPT. It uses eight inner folds to validate those configurations. Most non-foundation methods also predict with an eight-fold cross-validation ensemble. So “default” does not mean one unensembled fit. Foundation-model refitting follows a different procedure. And TabM’s internal shared-parameter ensemble from L054 is yet another layer: it can sit inside each fit, before any outer bagging or post-hoc ensembling. Paper §2.1.

In plain terms. Nested cross-validation is two nested "hold something out" loops. The outer loop hides a test set that nobody is allowed to peek at. The inner loop, working only inside the remaining data, hides small validation folds so the procedure can choose its own settings honestly. The outer test then judges whatever the inner loop chose.

Nested cross-validation separates two jobs. The outer split reserves a test set. Inside the remaining data, each inner fit trains on seven of eight parts and predicts the eighth. Combining those held-out predictions lets the procedure select a configuration or an ensemble without inspecting the outer test. For a bagged method, average the eight fitted predictors on the outer-test rows, then measure error.

Worked example — where 816 comes from. The paper uses repeated three-fold outer CV. Datasets below 2,500 samples get ten repetitions; the rest get three. That gives 30 or 9 outer splits per dataset. The four archived artifacts have 17 datasets with 30 splits and 34 with 9, so 17×30 + 34×9 = 816. Neither 816 nor the eight inner fits is a count of independent datasets. Paper §2.3.

Ask what budget is being held equal. Two hundred configurations equalizes the candidate count. It does not equalize seconds, hardware, energy, or inference latency. The paper limits each configuration evaluation to one hour on one training split, and records the hardware used. Its AutoGluon reference pipeline has a separate four-hour recipe. A peak-performance ranking and a latency-constrained deployment ranking answer different questions. This lab inspects cached scores; it does not retune on test or remeasure timing.

Why a family name is too coarse for attribution¶

One name, many procedures. “XGBoost” can mean a default configuration, the best validation-selected candidate from a search, a bagged ensemble, or a component in a larger stack. Those are different fitted procedures with different costs. The same applies to a neural architecture with preprocessing variants and multiple seeds.

Worked example. Imagine procedure A trains one default model for two minutes. Procedure B searches 100 candidates, selects ten, and averages them. If B wins, the result supports the complete B procedure under its resource allocation. It does not isolate the effect of one base architecture. A separate controlled experiment is needed for that attribution.

Search cost and serving cost are different axes. A heavily tuned single model can be cheap to serve after expensive development. A lightly tuned large ensemble can be expensive on every prediction. Record training/search cost, final model size, and prediction cost separately, so the result can inform the intended use case.

Preprocessing belongs in the identity. Two methods may both use an MLP backbone, while one adds learned feature embeddings, a different numerical transform, and a stronger optimizer recipe. A benchmark taxonomy should describe those choices, rather than treating the backbone name as the whole method.

Preserve full names when comparing. Short labels are useful in a plot, but the caption or linked manifest should map each label to the exact fitted recipe. Otherwise an attractive ranking figure becomes hard to interpret precisely.

Trace a prediction through the nested procedure¶

Set up the symbols. Let the outer training pool contain N rows, and let the untouched outer test have Q rows. Take one candidate configuration c. Partition the N rows into eight inner validation sets. Inner fit j then receives about 7N/8 rows and produces two arrays: predictions on its held-out N/8 rows, and predictions on all Q outer-test rows. For C-class classification these have shapes approximately [N/8,C] and [Q,C]. For regression there is one output per row. Scatter the held-out predictions back into their original row positions to build an out-of-fold (OOF) array [N,C]. An out-of-fold prediction for a row comes from a fit that excluded that row from weight fitting. Its label can still be used for validation and early stopping, so OOF does not mean an untouched final test.

From OOF errors to a final prediction. Compute the validation error on this OOF array. The tuned procedure chooses the best configuration c* using those errors. It then averages the eight test-prediction arrays of c* to form a [Q,C] prediction. This is a cross-validation ensemble, also called bagging here; its members differ only in which rows they were fitted on. A post-hoc ensemble does something different: it combines several candidate configurations by selecting nonnegative weights on their OOF predictions, then applies those same weights to their bagged test predictions. Order matters here. Averaging probabilities must come before taking the log loss; averaging eight log losses is not the loss of the average prediction. Only after selection is finished do you compare the resulting test predictions to the Q test labels.

Worked example — why the order matters. Two members predict probabilities .2 and .8 for a positive test label. Their mean prediction is .5, whose log loss is −log(.5)≈.693. Their individual losses average to [−log(.2)−log(.8)]/2≈.916. The second number evaluates randomly selecting a member, not averaging its probability. Nothing guarantees a particular ensemble improves every dataset, but the object being measured must be the intended predictor.

Do not confuse the three ensemble axes. They stack on top of each other. TabM-mini's k=32 members share one neural backbone within a single fit. Eight-fold bagging trains eight such predictors on different inner row sets. Post-hoc ensembling combines configurations that have different hyperparameters. Cross-family ensembling in L057 adds a fourth axis by combining different model families. Figure 6 in TabArena uses a learned cross-family portfolio, selected with leave-one-dataset-out evaluation, size 200 and 40 ensemble selection steps (Appendix C.3). Our four-family score files cannot reconstruct those ensemble predictions, because metric errors carry no information about which individual rows each model got wrong.

The exact wrapper changes what a family name means¶

Two preprocessing layers. Appendix C.1 separates common feature handling from model-specific preprocessing. Common processing handles text/datetime columns, retains numerical values, preserves categorical identity while coding categories, drops constant/duplicate columns, and passes missingness onward. Model-specific handling then decides scaling, encoding, and imputation. Applying a single numerical scaler to every family would not reproduce this design.

The details that matter for our four names. RealMLP is the CPU pipeline; it handles missing numerical values with mean imputation plus an indicator, and its default disables label smoothing because the benchmark scores AUC/log loss rather than accuracy. TabM_GPU uses numerical quantile preprocessing and its own missingness handling; Table C.8 specifies tabm-mini, k=32, piecewise-linear numerical embeddings, and clipping norm 1.0. That is not the numeric small-budget L054 lab configuration. CatBoost and LightGBM rely on their integrated implementations for model-specific processing. These statements describe the paper's recipes; the score files cannot independently prove which operations produced each stored error.

Foundation models take a different branch. After validation, they refit/use the complete outer training pool as context instead of eight-fold bagging, following author recommendations. Their applicability limits and checkpoints matter. The TabICL v1.1 checkpoint and TabDPT 1.1 used here differ from older paper versions, and neither receives the 200-configuration HPO search. Do not extend an eight-inner-fit diagram to these methods without drawing the refit branch.

What the one-hour limit covers. It covers a configuration's entire eight-inner-fit evaluation (Appendix A.2), not one hour for each of eight fits.

Scope check. Hardware affects which procedure is even attainable. Appendix A.3 reports that around 16% of CPU TabM/ModernNCA runs reached that time cap, versus less than .1% on GPU. So equal candidate counts plus different hardware do not isolate architecture alone. Appendix A.2 also records EBM exceeding the intended time limit — a real exception to audit, rather than silently erase. The AutoGluon 1.3 best-quality four-hour reference follows separate constraints.

Source: §2.1, Appendix A.2–A.3, and Appendix C.

4. Follow one error into the aggregate

Once procedures and coverage match, reduce their measurements. Ranking within a dataset/split handles incomparable error scales; averaging within datasets before across them controls the unit of influence. These are substantive evaluation choices.

In plain terms. A raw score on one dataset cannot be added to a raw score on another; they are in different units. The fix is to turn scores into ranks within each dataset, then average the ranks. That way each dataset gets one equal vote, no matter how large its numbers happen to be.

First, align the evaluation key. The evaluation key is the tuple that identifies what is being compared: dataset, outer split, method, and regime. Every comparison must refer to the same held-out rows and the same metric. Binary ROC-AUC measures how well positives are ordered above negatives, and larger is better, so the error is 1−AUC. Multiclass log loss penalizes low probability on the true class. Regression RMSE is the square root of the mean squared prediction error. Smaller is better for both of those. The artifact’s metric_error column already stores errors in this orientation. Do not subtract it from one again.

Ranks make different metrics comparable. RMSE has target units, so averaging its values across unrelated datasets — or mixing it with log loss — is not meaningful. Compare models within each dataset/split, where the units match, and convert to ranks. Rank 1 is best. Worked example. For the error vector [.4, .2, .2, .9], the tied second and third entries occupy positions 1 and 2, so the ranks are [3, 1.5, 1.5, 4]. Average ties; never let input order break them.

r(d,s,m) = 1 + count(errors < e) + (count(errors = e) − 1)/2
R(d,m) = sum_s r(d,s,m) / S(d)
R(m) = sum_d R(d,m) / D

Read the three lines in order. Here d identifies a dataset, s its outer split, m a method, e that method’s error, S(d) the dataset’s number of splits, and D the number of datasets. First form a split rank. Then average those ranks inside each dataset. Then average the dataset summaries. This is what gives each dataset equal influence.

Predict: if the small dataset is repeated three times with identical ranks, should that alone strengthen its influence? Move the control. The two dataset identities and all their errors stay fixed.

Pairwise wins are a second summary of the same ranks. A pairwise win records 1 if A’s error is lower, 0 if higher, and ½ for an exact tie. Average those wins over splits inside each dataset, then over datasets. On a complete common grid of M methods, a method’s win total against the others is exactly M − rank. Worked example. For rank 1.5 among four methods, that is 4 − 1.5 = 2.5 wins. Our implementation matches this operation in the pinned upstream evaluator.

Missingness changes the claim. Dropping failed evaluations may leave an easier dataset subset behind. Filling a missing result with a baseline represents a fallback system, not the missing model’s measured performance. Our lab therefore rejects missing keys, duplicate keys, inconsistent metrics, and imputed rows. A legitimate subset analysis should name its common task set and its exclusion rule before looking at any winners.

Walk from repetitions to one dataset vote¶

Worked example — one dataset's vote. Suppose model A has losses 0.20 and 0.24 on two repetitions of dataset 1, while B has 0.23 and 0.23. Their dataset means are 0.22 and 0.23, so A ranks first on that dataset. Dataset 2 may use a different loss scale, so rank there separately, after the declared within-dataset aggregation.

The order of operations matters. Ranking every seed and then averaging the ranks need not equal averaging the losses and then ranking the means. Worked example. For A losses (0.10,0.50,0.50) and B losses (0.40,0.40,0.40), A wins only one of three repetitions but has the lower mean loss, about 0.367 versus 0.400. The two summaries answer different questions.

Ties receive average positions. If three models have losses (0.2,0.2,0.5), their ranks are (1.5,1.5,3). Assigning ranks (1,2,3) by column order would invent a distinction that the metric never expressed. Rounded published scores can also hide genuine differences, which is one more reason to prefer saved predictions or unrounded scores for reconstruction.

Relative-error transforms need an explicit denominator. Dividing by a baseline error asks how much loss remains relative to that baseline. Subtracting the best error and scaling by a range asks a different question. Small or zero denominators can make a normalized score unstable. Inspect the formula and its edge-case policy before interpreting any unitless axis.

Ranks discard magnitude. A method that wins by 0.0001 on four tasks and loses by 0.2 on one can have an attractive mean rank but an undesirable deployment profile. Keep task-level effects, failure cases, and practical tolerances beside the ordinal summaries.

Reconstruct the appendix's alternative summaries¶

Average rank and harmonic mean rank. Appendix A.1 first averages errors across outer folds. On each resulting dataset vector, it reports several summaries with different purposes. Average rank rewards consistent ordinal strength. Harmonic mean rank is D divided by the sum of reciprocal ranks. Worked example. Ranks [1,5] give 2/(1+1/5)=1.667, versus an arithmetic mean of 3. This emphasizes occasional very strong performance, which may motivate studying ensemble contribution; it does not prove useful prediction diversity.

Normalized score and improvability. The normalized score places the best error b at 1 and the median error h at 0: max(0,(h−e)/(h−b)). Worked example. For errors [.2,.3,.5], h=.3 and b=.2, so the scores are [1,0,0]. This discards differences below the median and depends on the competitor pool. If h=b, the formula is undefined, so an implementation must specify a policy. Improvability instead uses 100(e−b)/e. Worked example. At e=.25 and b=.20, 20% of that method's error is removable by switching to the measured best. With zero error, its division also needs an explicit edge policy. Neither statistic is an accuracy-point gain.

The new rating exercise computes both orders from the same artifacts: split comparisons averaged by dataset, and comparisons of dataset-mean errors. Everything else is fixed. This is a controlled analysis intervention, not new training. Predict which statistics must agree (coverage, raw dataset errors) and which need not (ranks, pairwise frequencies, ratings). Appendix A.1's written mean-error order and our pinned current evaluator's split-aware path are separately labeled; matching one cannot certify the other.

Source: Appendix A.1, aggregation definitions and Table A.1.

5. Read Elo without calling it accuracy

Mean rank and Elo both compare relative performance, but they use different constructions and depend on the comparison pool. Understanding the reduction above prevents a rating difference from being misread as an accuracy difference.

In plain terms. An Elo rating is a single number per method, borrowed from chess. The only thing it promises is a prediction of head-to-head matchups: how often one method beats another. A rating gap of 50 or 400 is not a gap in accuracy percentage points — it is a statement about the odds of winning the next matchup.

Elo expresses relative strength through pairwise outcomes. A Bradley–Terry model fits one strength number per method, so that rating differences predict expected wins. The paper uses a scale where a 400-point gap corresponds to 10:1 odds, and it anchors the default random forest at 1000. Adding the same constant to every rating leaves every predicted matchup unchanged. The anchor is what fixes that otherwise-arbitrary origin. Paper §2.3.

odds(A beats B) = 10^((E_A − E_B)/400)
p(A beats B) = odds / (1 + odds)

Worked example. E_A and E_B are ratings. At equal ratings, odds=1 and p=½. At a 400-point gap, odds=10 and p=10/11≈.909. That .909 is expected pairwise benchmark success under the rating model, not 90.9% correct labels. It is also not guaranteed to equal every observed pair’s win frequency.

All three ordinal summaries discard the margin. Rank, win rate, and Elo throw away much of the error margin: winning by .0001 and winning by .1 can count equally. The lab now reconstructs ranks, pairwise wins, and fitted Bradley–Terry ratings, and compares observed matchups with the fitted probabilities. The current-source parity check verifies this local evaluator only, not the historical Figure 1 pool and not the original training.

Elo is a model of relative outcomes¶

The rating is just a link function. An Elo-style rating maps rating differences to expected pairwise win probabilities under a specified link function. In the common base-10 form, the expected probability that A beats B is 1/[1+10^((R_B−R_A)/400)]. Equal ratings imply 0.5; a 400-point advantage implies about 0.909. The scale constant is a convention, not a unit of prediction accuracy.

The "games" are derived from task scores. Their definition, their tie treatment, their weighting, and the fitting procedure all determine what the ratings summarize. A 50-point rating gain cannot be read as a five-percentage-point accuracy gain. It also does not specify the magnitude of loss reduction on any particular dataset.

A rating pool is relational. Changing the opponents or the included tasks can change the fitted ratings. This is appropriate for a relative summary, but it means ratings from differently constructed pools are not automatically interchangeable.

Resample whole datasets, not scores. For uncertainty, resample whole dataset blocks jointly across models. Preserve all paired observations belonging to a selected block, according to the declared estimator. Resampling individual model scores independently breaks the shared difficulty structure; treating folds as unrelated tasks can overstate task diversity.

Interpret an interval according to what was resampled¶

An interval only covers what you resampled. A dataset bootstrap interval describes sensitivity to the observed task collection under the bootstrap model. It does not include uncertainty from unobserved model implementations, alternative tuning budgets, or an entirely different deployment population. A seed interval on fixed rows describes an even narrower source of variation.

When two methods are close, say so. Report that the local evidence cannot resolve their ordering reliably. A leaderboard must display an order, but a scientific interpretation does not have to pretend that every adjacent position is meaningful. The exit audit should identify which differences are robust, which are practically small, and which depend on aggregation choices.

Fit a rating from observed outcomes¶

Step 1 — build the matchup matrix. For dataset d and split s, let w(d,s,i,j) be 1 when method i has lower error than j, 0 when higher, and .5 for a tie. Average over splits to obtain W[d,i,j], a [D,M,M] tensor: one complete matchup matrix per dataset. A displayed diagonal of .5 means a self-tie, and the fitter excludes self-games. Reciprocity requires W[d,i,j]+W[d,j,i]=1. Sum over datasets to obtain C[i,j]. Each pair then has D total contests, even though some datasets had more repeated folds.

Step 2 — model the win probability. Give each method a log-strength t_i, and model its win probability as p_ij=1/(1+exp(−(t_i−t_j))). The observed fraction is C[i,j]/D; the fitted probability is a prediction of the rating model. The two may differ, because one strength per method must explain all pairs at once. A circular pattern of pairwise advantages cannot be matched exactly by a single scalar ordering.

Step 3 — the likelihood and its gradient. For each unordered pair i<j, the negative log-likelihood is D·log(1+exp(t_i−t_j))−C[i,j]·(t_i−t_j). This is binary cross-entropy, written stably with logaddexp. Its derivative with respect to the strength difference is expected wins minus observed wins, D·p_ij−C[i,j]. A positive residual says the model predicts too many i wins, so optimization lowers i relative to j. Sum those contributions over opponents. The visible lab solver uses this objective and analytic gradient, rather than importing an opaque Elo evaluator.

Step 4 — two numerical issues. First, shifting all t values equally changes no probabilities, so we centre the ratings at mean 1000 after fitting. The paper instead anchors default RandomForest at 1000; that arm is absent from our pool. Second, if a method wins every contest, an unpenalized maximum can run to infinite strength. The pinned current implementation adds λ·sum(t_i²), with λ=.5/(10⁶·ln(10)²); our fitter uses the same disclosed tiny ridge. That stabilizes separated data, but it means this is a penalized fit, not an unrestricted maximum-likelihood estimate.

Step 5 — convert and sanity-check. Convert strengths using E_i=1000+400(t_i−mean(t))/ln(10). Check the two-method case before trusting an optimizer. Worked example. Three wins and one loss imply odds 3, a rating gap of 400·log10(3)≈190.85, and a fitted probability of .75, apart from the tiny ridge effect. The fifth live TODO builds W; the provided solver consumes it on all 51 real datasets. So a changed TODO changes the reported ratings and bootstrap contrasts.

What the new execution verifies¶

The extension compares its fitted four-method ratings with the pinned current upstream EloHelper.compute_mle_elo_from_ranks on every regime. It also removes two opponents and refits: the observed CatBoost/TabM frequency stays fixed, but the fitted rating gap can change because other matchups no longer constrain the two strengths. Mean 1000 is re-established in each pool; compare gaps, not absolute levels across pools.

For uncertainty we draw 100 whole-dataset bootstrap samples, refit each sample, and subtract CatBoost's rating in that same draw. This preserves covariance between methods. The interval is a percentile interval for a rating contrast, distinct from the older 2,000-draw interval for a mean rank contrast. One hundred draws mirror the paper's stated bootstrap count but provide coarse tail resolution; this does not restore its roster, historical weighting, solver or anchor. The source file documents later tolerance changes and the ridge already present in its logistic-regression path. Our new JSON is versioned separately and the historical rank results are retained.

There is a useful same-paper check: Appendix A.1/Table A.1 lists RealMLP (tuned + ensembled) at 1569 and TabM at 1552 on its imputed full roster. Section 3.1 describes the Figure 1 ranking with a different roster. Read the exact figure/table and included methods before saying “the paper winner”; do not assume one ranking applies to every panel. A roster difference is a possible source of a changed ordering, not by itself a numerical reconstruction of why it changed.

The official reconstruction command targets a camera-ready collection at a later repository revision. The initial paper, that collection, the public current leaderboard and this four-method audit are four different evidence identities. To claim exact recovery, reconcile their versions, opponent/regime pool, aggregation order, missing-result fallback, anchor and bootstrap before comparing a number. Running a current solver successfully settles only the current-solver question.

Statistics extension: distinguish the order of operations

Following the Demšar multiple-dataset comparison framework, for the course’s Friedman/Nemenyi summary, first average errors over outer splits inside each dataset, then rank those mean errors within each dataset. This differs from averaging split ranks above. Example: A’s two errors are [0,100], B’s [1,2]. Split ranks average to a tie; mean errors rank B first. Do not combine one summary’s ranks with another summary’s statistical test.

The Friedman test uses datasets as blocks and asks whether methods have consistent rank differences; its p-value is not a probability that the methods are equal. Nemenyi’s critical difference (CD) is a threshold for pairwise differences in mean ranks, using CD=q·√(M(M+1)/(6D)), with q from the studentized-range distribution divided by √2. For D=51, M=4 and level .05, CD=.657. The ensembled Friedman p=.0309 is unadjusted across three regimes; at a conservative .05/3=.0167 threshold it does not pass.
