The Deep-Tabular Landscape & rtdl

Lesson 041 · Phase 2 · Q1 · ~50 min · primary reading: Gorishniy et al. 2021, "Revisiting Deep Learning Models for Tabular Data"

Year 1 is closed. You built a high, regenerable single-table bar — a leak-free, tuned gradient-boosted tree ensemble under the L020 fair-comparison contract — and you wrote, cold, why that bar is stubborn (Grinsztajn's three inductive biases, L025–L027). You also watched a quarter's worth of single-table upgrades — honest MLP/ResNet nets (L028), AutoML (L029), entity embeddings (L031), a TabTransformer preview (L032) — repeatedly tie or fail against that bar (the exhaustion cascade).

Year 2 does not abandon that discipline; it weaponises it. The goal of the year: know every major neural tabular architecture and tabular foundation model, know when each wins, and know exactly why strong trees and MLPs still often win on single tables. This is the year that makes you honest about neural baselines — so that when a relational model eventually claims a win in Years 3–4, the single-table opponent it beats is the strongest fair one, not a strawman.

Lesson 041 is the map and the toolkit for that year. The opener is deliberately a methods paper, not the flashiest architecture: Gorishniy, Rubachev, Khrulkov & Babenko (NeurIPS 2021), Revisiting Deep Learning Models for Tabular Data. Its contribution is not one model but a correction to the field: there was no strong, simple, shared baseline, so a lot of reported "deep learning beats trees on tables" progress was measured against weak or unequally-tuned opponents. The paper gives us three things we will lean on all year: a strong ResNet baseline, a strong universal FT-Transformer, and an honest headline — no universal winner against a tuned GBDT.

Its single skill: read the neural-tabular literature as a map anchored by a fair protocol and two reference models (a ResNet baseline + FT-Transformer), so that any "DL beats GBDT" claim is judged against a strong, equally-tuned baseline — not a strawman — and set up the rtdl reference implementations you will train from Lesson 042 on.

One sentence. Before you believe any deep-tabular result, you need a strong simple baseline (ResNet) and a shared tuning protocol; Gorishniy 2021 supplies both, adds FT-Transformer as the strong universal model, and still finds no architecture that universally beats a tuned GBDT.

Why "revisiting"? The baseline problem

A recurring pattern in the 2017–2021 deep-tabular literature: a paper proposes an architecture, reports that it beats "the MLP" and some trees, and claims deep learning has arrived on tables. The catch, named by Gorishniy 2021, is that these comparisons were not on equal footing — baselines were often weak, differently preprocessed, or unequally tuned, and there was no shared, tested implementation to compare against. When each paper brings its own baseline, the numbers are not comparable, and apparent progress can be a tuning-effort gap wearing a model-quality mask.

You have seen this exact failure mode from the reviewer's side: L038's meaningful vs naive baseline and HP-budget parity, and L023's corrected tests. Gorishniy 2021 is that discipline applied to a whole subfield: fix a strong baseline, fix a shared tuning protocol, then re-run everyone.

Strong simple baseline. A model that is cheap and unglamorous but, when tuned under the same protocol as the fancy model, is genuinely hard to beat. Gorishniy's is a well-tuned ResNet (an MLP with residual connections). The claim that stings: it alone matches many published "novel" architectures — so it must be run first.
Shared tuning protocol. One fixed recipe for hyper-parameter search (search space, budget, optimiser, validation split, metric) applied identically to every model. Without it, "model A beat model B" may only mean "A got more Optuna trials." This is L017's fair-budget rule, promoted to a field standard.

The landscape — families of neural tabular models

Here is the whole Year-2 Q1 syllabus as a map. Each chip is a model family we will train or read critically over Lessons 042–049; the grey GBDT chip is the Year-1 bar they are all measured against. Click through them — the default is ResNet, the baseline Gorishniy says to run before believing anything else. Notice the map is organised by mechanism (baseline · attention · tree-inspired · feature crosses · incumbent), not by publication hype.

Reading the map: every family is an attempt to buy back one of the tree inductive biases (irregular targets, privileged columns, junk robustness) with a differentiable mechanism — soft trees (NODE), sparse masks (TabNet), attention (FT-Transformer/SAINT), explicit crosses (DCNv2) — while staying trainable by gradient descent. Whether any of them clears the fair GBDT bar is the year's question.

Two models that anchor the year

Of the ten chips, two are load-bearing because Gorishniy 2021 establishes them as the reference points the rest of the year uses: the ResNet baseline and FT-Transformer.

ResNet you already met in L028: an MLP whose blocks add a residual (skip) connection, output = x + f(x), so a deep stack can represent the identity easily and gradients flow without vanishing. Nothing exotic — the point is that tuned under a shared protocol it is strong, and it is the honest floor every attention model must clear.

FT-Transformer (Feature Tokenizer + Transformer) is the paper's headline architecture and the one Q1 builds toward (full treatment in L046). Its one new idea over the TabTransformer you previewed is the Feature Tokenizer: it turns every feature into an embedding token — including numeric ones — where TabTransformer only embedded categoricals and let numbers bypass the attention.

Feature Tokenizer. The layer that makes a row look like a sentence of tokens the Transformer can attend over. A numeric feature x_j becomes a vector T_j = x_j · W_j + b_j (one learned weight vector W_j and bias per column, so the single number is scaled into an embedding); a categorical value becomes an embedding-table lookup (as in L031). Every column now has its own d-dimensional token, numeric and categorical alike.
[CLS] token. A single extra learnable token prepended to the sequence (borrowed from BERT). It carries no input feature; after the Transformer layers, its output vector is a pooled summary of the whole row, and the prediction head reads only it. It is how a set of per-feature tokens is turned back into one prediction.

The tokenized row that FT-Transformer feeds to its Transformer stack looks like this:

row = [ age=39, hours=40, workclass="Gov", education="HS" ]
tokens = [ [CLS], Tok(age), Tok(hours), Emb(workclass), Emb(education) ]
→ L× Transformer self-attention layers → read [CLS] → linear head → ŷ

Why tokenising numerics matters: in TabTransformer a continuous column can only influence the output through the final MLP, never through attention, so numeric–categorical interactions are weak. FT-T lets age and workclass attend to each other directly. That is the architectural reason FT-Transformer is the stronger, more universal classic neural model — and why L046 spends a full lesson on it.

The honest headline — no universal winner

Before the reveal, commit a prediction. This is the pretesting effect: guessing first, even wrong, makes the result stick.

Gorishniy 2021's re-run, under one shared protocol on a broad suite, lands on a conclusion that should feel familiar from L024 and your L039 synthesis — reached here from the architecture side rather than the inductive-bias side:

Finding (Gorishniy 2021)What it means for us
A tuned ResNet is a strong baseline that matches many prior "novel" DL architectures. Run ResNet before believing any new model. A win over a weak MLP is not a win (L038).
FT-Transformer is the most universally good DL model — best or near-best DL across the suite. It is the neural single-table bar to reproduce (L046) — the strongest classic net an RDL result must eventually beat fairly.
No universal winner vs GBDT. GBDT still wins on a large share of datasets; DL wins on others; the ranking is dataset-dependent. Same verdict as Grinsztajn, from architecture design: single-table cleverness narrows the gap but does not close it universally (M50).
Fair tuning and ensembling move results as much as architecture choice. Protocol is not a footnote — it is the experiment. This is why the year fixes one shared protocol.
No universal winner. There is no single tabular architecture that beats all others on all datasets — the best model is dataset-dependent, and a tuned GBDT remains a top contender. "DL is finally better on tables" is not what a fair re-run shows; "we now have a strong, reproducible DL baseline, and it ties or trades wins with GBDT" is.

rtdl — the toolkit for the year

rtdl ("Research on Tabular Deep Learning") is the code released with the paper: tested, reference PyTorch implementations of the MLP, the ResNet baseline, and FT-Transformer, plus the preprocessing and tuning conventions the paper used. We use it for exactly the reason the paper exists — so our neural baselines are strong and standard, not something we half-implemented and then beat by accident. A win against a reference implementation means something; a win against your own buggy MLP does not.

The maintained package is rtdl (the FT-Transformer/ResNet models live in the rtdl_revisiting_models package on PyPI). The Lesson 042 lab trains a ResNet baseline with it; today's setup just gets it importing.

Thesis bridge

Why a methods paper opens Year 2. The mission's eventual claim is comparative: a relational model, keeping structure the flatten destroys, beats the fair single-table bar. Year 1 made the tree half of that bar honest and strong. Year 2 makes the neural half honest and strong — because a skeptic will say "you only beat GBDT; a good tabular transformer would have won on a flat table too." FT-Transformer and rtdl are how we make sure that when we run the comparison, the single-table DL opponent is the best fair one. Beating a strawman net would be as worthless as beating an undertuned XGBoost.

Lab — set up rtdl

This is an environment-setup station, not a bake-off; the training starts in L042. Goal: get the reference models importing and a smoke-test forward pass running, so nothing about your neural baseline is home-made.

  1. In the labs environment, install the reference models: pip install rtdl_revisiting_models (plus torch if not present). Record the resolved versions in your run manifest (L037 discipline).
  2. Import and instantiate all three: MLP, ResNet, and FTTransformer. Run one forward pass on a small random batch with a handful of numeric + categorical features; confirm the output shape matches the number of targets.

  4. Write one sentence in your notes: what does rtdl give you that re-implementing an MLP from scratch would not? (Answer to check against: a strong, standard baseline, so a later win is credible.)
