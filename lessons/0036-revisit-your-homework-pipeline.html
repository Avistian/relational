Revisit Your Homework Pipeline

Lesson 036 · Phase 1 · Q4 · ~50 min · primary reading: your own homework/report.md, read as a reviewer

Thirty-five lessons of this curriculum have been spent learning to distrust other people's pipelines: the single-table assumption (L001), leaky design matrices (L002), folds that don't respect groups (L004), preprocessing fitted outside the fold (L005), comparisons that mistake noise for a win (L023). This lesson turns those instruments around and points them at your own code — the ReAction L&D response-prediction submission sitting in ~/Projects/homework, written before this curriculum began.

This is not a formality. That submission is careful work: it uses StratifiedGroupKFold grouped by person_id, it asserts the absence of person leakage on all 25 folds, it prefers calibration over accuracy, it refuses pseudo-labelling on principle, it pre-registers numeric ship-gates, and it documents what it deliberately did not do. It is better than most production pipelines. And it still contains four findings worth naming — one of which changed which model got deployed.

Its single skill: audit a pipeline against the Q1–Q3 leakage spine, then triage each finding by what it actually changes — the number you reported, the artifact you shipped, the decision you made, or nothing but a caveat you owe the reader — and re-measure before you believe any of it.

One sentence. "I found a leak" is not a verdict but a hypothesis with a consequence, and the consequences are genuinely different: a statistic fitted outside the fold inflates the number you reported, a mis-split inside the training fold leaves your number honest but ships a worse artifact, an argmin over a handful of correlated folds changes your decision while every metric stays correct, and a missing column can mean a defect you can only declare — so an audit's real output is a ranked list of consequences with a measurement attached to each, not a count of smells.

First, the vocabulary (nothing here is assumed)

An audit is only as sharp as the words you bring to it. Everything this lesson relies on is defined here from first principles, including terms earlier lessons already used.

Audit. Reading a finished pipeline against a fixed checklist to find the gap between what it claims and what it does. It is distinct from debugging (which starts from a symptom) and from testing (which checks intended behaviour): an audit starts from a rubric and is willing to conclude "this is fine."
The leakage spine. This workspace's name for the ordered set of questions that decide whether a reported number is real, accumulated across Q1–Q3: is the split honest for the dependence structure in the data (L003/L004), is every preprocessing statistic fitted inside the fold (L005), is each feature knowable at prediction time (L002/L022), is the metric the one the decision needs (L008), and is the comparison powered enough to support the claim (L023)?
Group (cluster) leakage. When rows are not independent — several situations belonging to one person — and the same group appears in both training and test. The model then gets credit for recognising a person it has already met (their personality scores, engagement, wearable coverage) rather than for generalising to a new one. The fix is a grouped splitter: StratifiedGroupKFold(groups=person_id) keeps every row of a person on one side.
Nested split. Any second split taken inside a fold of the first one — for calibration, early stopping, feature selection, hyper-parameter search, or to train a helper model that manufactures a feature. The crucial property: a nested split is still a split, so every discipline you apply to the outer one (grouping, time ordering) applies to it too. Library defaults do not inherit your grouping.
Calibration, and isotonic calibration. A model is calibrated when events it calls "70 %" happen about 70 % of the time. A raw classifier's scores are usually not, so you fit a calibrator: a function from raw score to honest probability. Isotonic calibration fits a non-decreasing step function — flexible, non-parametric, and the standard pairing with boosted trees. Crucially it must be fitted on data the base model did not train on; otherwise it learns to trust scores that are sharper than they will ever be again.
ECE (expected calibration error). Bin predictions by confidence (here 15 equal-width bins on the top-class probability); in each bin compare mean confidence to observed accuracy; average the absolute gaps weighted by bin population: ECE = Σ_b (|B_b|/N)·|acc(B_b) − conf(B_b)|. Zero is perfect. It is the number an EU auditor reads, and the submission's ship-gate threshold is 0.05 globally, 0.07 per slice.
Log-loss, and why it is the primary metric. LL = −(1/N)·Σ log p(true class). It is a strictly proper scoring rule: uniquely minimised by reporting the true posterior, so it penalises bad ranking and bad calibration together. Reference point for 5 balanced-ish classes: predicting the uniform prior gives −log(1/5) ≈ 1.6094, so anything above that is worse than an honest shrug.
Transductive vs. inductive. An inductive procedure produces a rule usable on a row that does not exist yet. A transductive one uses the unlabelled rows it will be scored on while building the rule. Fitting an imputer's median or a scaler's mean on the whole table is transductive: no label leaks, but the number you report is no longer a deployment estimate, because at inference time that statistic could not have been computed.
Selection bias (the winner's curse). Score k models on the same folds and take the best: the maximum of several noisy estimates is optimistically biased, and the more models and the noisier the folds, the larger the bias. This is why the score that chose a model is not a fair estimate of that model, and why nested CV (L017) exists.
Corrected resampled t-test (Nadeau & Bengio, 2003). Cross-validation folds share training data, so their scores are correlated and the naive paired t-test's variance, σ²/n, is too small — it declares wins too readily. The correction inflates it by (1/n + n_test/n_train). For 5 folds with ~1,117 test and ~4,470 train rows that is 0.200 + 0.250 = 0.450 against the naive 0.200 — more than double the variance, so the confidence interval widens by about √(0.450/0.200) ≈ 1.5×. Assigned in L023.

The pipeline under audit

The task: predict which of five behavioural outcomes follows an L&D recommendation (completed_effective, completed_ineffective, declined, dropped_out, partial), with calibrated probabilities, for an EU-facing high-risk HR deployment. Three tables — 20,000 persons, 119,498 situations, 5,587 responses — joined to one row per situation. Only 4.68 % of situations carry a label.

The dependence structure is the thing to hold on to, because every finding below turns on it. Persons recur: a person has up to 17 situations, and among the 5,587 labelled rows there are 4,851 distinct persons, of whom 675 contribute more than one labelled row — 1,411 rows, just over a quarter of the labelled set, belong to a person who appears again. That is exactly enough repetition to make grouping matter and little enough to make its effect small: both halves of that sentence get measured below.

Five models were compared on identical person-grouped folds: M0 logistic regression, M1 LightGBM + isotonic calibration, and three semi-supervised variants adding a KMeans block (M2a), a labelling-propensity feature (M2b), or both (M2c). M2a shipped.

Finding 1 — the split you didn't write

Consequence class · degrades the shipped artifact, leaves the reported number honest

The inner calibration split ignores person_id

The outer CV is person-grouped and asserted. But calibration takes a second split, and it is built from an integer: 

# src/modeling.py — M1, and therefore also M2a/M2b/M2c and the shipped model
def make_lgbm_calibrated():
    return CalibratedClassifierCV(estimator=_lgbm(), method="isotonic", cv=5)

Given cv=5 and a classifier, CalibratedClassifierCV builds a plain StratifiedKFold(5) over the rows it was handed — which are the rows of one outer training fold. It stratifies by the label. It has never heard of person_id, and there is no argument on that line through which it could. So inside the training block, one situation of a person can be used to fit the base LightGBM while another situation of the same person is used to fit the isotonic map.

Why that matters mechanically: the isotonic step function's whole job is to learn how much to trust the base model's scores, and it can only learn that from scores on rows the base model has not seen. Feed it a person the base model already trained on and those scores are sharper than the model will ever manage on a stranger, so the monotone map it fits is the wrong shape for the genuinely-unseen people it will meet in production.

Below: each capsule is a person, each square a situation. Blue squares fit the base model, amber squares fit the calibrator, red squares are the outer test fold. The widget opens on the library default; switch to the grouped inner split to see the difference.

Predict before you measure

This is the part of an audit where intuition usually fails, so commit to an answer first. You have found a genuine group leak inside the training fold. Which of the submission's numbers is wrong because of it?

The answer is none of them — and that is the finding, not an anticlimax. The leak lives entirely inside the outer training block. The outer test fold never enters the calibrator's fit, so the ECE and log-loss computed on it are honest measurements of the model that was actually built. What the mis-split damages is that model: it is fitted with a calibrator tuned against optimistically sharp scores. Your report was true; your artifact was worse than it needed to be. Most leakage lessons train you to expect an inflated metric, and that expectation would have made you look for this defect in the wrong place.

Re-measured, at full configuration

A finding is a hypothesis until it is measured. So: the submission's exact pipeline was re-run with one line changed — the inner cv=5 replaced by explicit person-grouped splits over the training fold — holding everything else fixed (same encoder, same 400-tree LightGBM, same outer folds, same seed, same metric code). 

# the fix: cv also accepts an ITERABLE of (train, test) index pairs
inner = list(StratifiedGroupKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
             .split(X[tr], y.iloc[tr], groups=groups[tr]))
model = CalibratedClassifierCV(estimator=_lgbm(), method="isotonic", cv=inner)

The baseline arm reproduced the submission's published M1 row exactly — log-loss 1.4248 ± 0.0392 and top-label ECE 0.0363 ± 0.0053, matching report.md §3.1 and the saved per-fold CSV to four decimals — so the difference between the two arms is attributable to the fix and not to the environment.

Read that honestly. Both numbers move in the direction the theory predicts — the properly-calibrated model is very slightly better on both — and both movements are far inside the noise: the log-loss gain is 0.0016 against a fold-to-fold standard deviation of 0.039, about a twenty-fifth of one fold's spread. The ECE gain is 0.0003 while its own fold spread widened from 0.0053 to 0.0094. On this dataset, at this label density, the defect is real and its measured cost is negligible.

Which raises the honest question: then why fix it? Three reasons, and none of them is the size of today's delta. First, the fix is one line and the exposure is not: only 675 of 4,851 labelled persons repeat now, and that ratio is a property of a 4.68 % label rate, not of the code — the same line behaves differently the moment more labels arrive, which the deployment memo plans for weekly. Second, a defect whose current cost is inside the noise is still a defect you cannot defend in the EU review the submission is written for; "we measured it and it was 0.0003" is a defensible sentence, and "we didn't notice" is not. Third, and most useful: the discipline generalises. Calibration is not the only nested split in the file, as the next finding shows.

Finding 2 — the number was right and the decision was wrong

Consequence class · changes the decision, leaves every measurement correct

M2a was shipped over M1 on a margin smaller than fold noise

Five models, one set of folds, winner by lowest mean log-loss. M2a won by 0.0032 nats.

Two further things fall out of re-reading the selection code rather than the report.

The stated tie-break rule never fired. report.md §3.4 explains the choice as: "lowest mean log-loss whose ECE does not exceed the loser by more than +0.02 … M2a's ECE (0.0332) is lower than M1's (0.0363), so the rule promotes M2a." But the implementation starts from the log-loss argmin and only ever replaces it with a worse-log-loss candidate that is dramatically better-calibrated: 

def pick_winner(cmp_df):
    s = cmp_df.sort_values('log_loss').reset_index(drop=True)
    best = s.iloc[0]                      # M2a is already here — the argmin
    for _, cand in s.iloc[1:].iterrows():
        if cand['ece_top'] + 0.02 < best['ece_top']:   # never true: 0.0296 vs 0.0332
            best = cand
    return best['model']

M2a was the log-loss argmin, so it was s.iloc[0] before the ECE clause was consulted, and no model's ECE was 0.02 better than its 0.0332 (the closest, M2c, is 0.0296 — a 0.0036 gap). The model was chosen by argmin alone. The calibration-first framing the report presents as the deciding rule did no work. This is worth more than the pedantry suggests: the report's narrative made a noise-driven choice look principled, which is precisely how such choices survive review.

The winner is not stable under dropping one fold. Recompute each model's mean log-loss with a single fold removed, five times:

Finding 3 — the encoder that saw everything

Consequence class · could inflate the reported number — the only class where the size must be measured, not argued

The ColumnTransformer is fitted once, on all 119,498 rows, before any CV

# notebooks/02_task2_modeling.ipynb, cell 3 — before the CV loop exists
pipe = make_feature_pipeline()
X_all = pipe.fit_transform(X_df).astype(np.float32)   # all 119,498 rows
X_lab = X_all[mask_labeled]                            # then sliced for CV
# ... and the same pattern for the SSL blocks:
unsup.fit(X_all)                       # KMeans centroids from every row, test folds included
propensity.fit(X_all, mask_labeled)     # and its inner OOF split is ungrouped too

The submission defends this explicitly, and the defence is half right: "the transformer is unsupervised, so using the unlabeled 95 % here is safe." No label leaks — that part is true, and it is why this is a smaller problem than it looks. But the medians, the scaler means and standard deviations, and the one-hot vocabulary were computed from rows that include every outer test fold. That makes the procedure transductive: it uses the data it is scored on. At inference time, on a situation that does not exist yet, those statistics could not have been computed — so the CV number is not the deployment number it is being read as.

The mechanism is the one from L005. The widget opens on the correct regime — the transformer refitted inside each fold, test cells never used for statistics. Click Fit on all data (leak) to see the submission's arrangement, where every cell including the test fold carries the ring.

Here the audit's discipline matters more than its suspicion. This is the one finding whose class allows the reported number to be optimistic, so it is the one where asserting the size would be unforgivable. Predict before you read the table: refitting the encoder inside every outer fold — on training-fold persons only, so no test person contributes a median, a scale, or a one-hot level — and holding everything else fixed, how much of the 1.4248 was borrowed?

None of it. The fold-honest encoder is not worse — it is 0.0011 nats better, and its ECE is 0.0013 better. That is 3 % of one fold's standard deviation, drifting in the direction opposite to the one the leakage story predicts, which is exactly what a real effect of zero looks like when measured with five folds. On 119,498 rows a median, a standard deviation and a one-hot vocabulary are simply not sensitive to whether a fifth of the persons are withheld; the statistics the encoder learns are converged long before the fold boundary matters.

The third row is the one worth staring at. Applying both fixes lands at 1.4352 — 0.0104 worse than the shipped arrangement, with the fold spread widening to 0.0499. That is not evidence that fixing leaks hurts. It is one fold: the combined arm scores 1.5237 on fold 1 where the shipped arm scores 1.4946, and the mechanism is the isotonic calibrator, not the leak. A monotone step function fitted on roughly 900 rows per inner fold can map a handful of test rows to a near-zero probability, and log-loss charges an unbounded price for every one of them — so the calibrator is itself a variance source, and re-drawing its inner splits re-rolls that dice. The honest summary of the whole table is therefore the range: 1.4232 to 1.4352, a spread smaller than one fold's standard deviation. At 5,587 labels over five folds, none of these four arrangements is distinguishable from the others, and the case for the two fixes has to be made on reproducibility and defensibility rather than on the metric. If you wanted to resolve differences this small you would need repeated CV — 5×5 folds, L023's protocol — and that is the honest answer to "which is best?", not a bolder reading of this table.

Sit with how uncomfortable that is, because it is the point of the whole lesson. The consequence class was correctly assigned — this arrangement genuinely can inflate a reported number, and the code is genuinely not reproducible at inference time — and the measured inflation is still nil. A class tells you where to look and what kind of damage is possible; only the re-measurement tells you the size, and "indistinguishable from zero" is a legitimate finding that you can only earn by running it. An auditor who stops at the class writes "leakage: CV estimate optimistic" and is wrong about this pipeline. An auditor who measures writes "transductive preprocessing, effect below fold noise, fix it for reproducibility rather than for the number" — and is trusted the next time the effect is large.

The reason to still care is the one the size of the number hides: the defence does not scale with the shortcut. "Unsupervised, therefore safe" is a claim about labels, not about transduction, and the same sentence gets attached to steps where it is far more expensive — a KMeans block whose centroids are placed using test rows (which is exactly what unsup.fit(X_all) does for the shipped model), or the propensity head, whose internal OOF split is a plain StratifiedKFold(5) with no groups argument, so a person's other situations help predict their own labelling probability. Each is defended by the same half-true sentence. The audit's job is to notice that the sentence is doing more work than it can bear, and then to price each use of it separately.

Priced, the KMeans one comes out the same way: fitting the 16 centroids inside each fold instead of on all 119,498 rows moved log-loss from 1.4271 to 1.4221 — again slightly better when honest, again inside fold noise. One caveat worth stating, because it is exactly the kind of thing an audit must not hide: that pair of runs uses a *reconstruction* of the semi-supervised block (cluster one-hots plus centroid distances) which does not reproduce the submission's published M2a row (1.4215), so the two numbers are comparable to each other and to nothing else. Finding 2's verdict does not lean on them — it is computed from the submission's own saved per-fold CSVs.

Finding 4 — the defect you can only declare

Consequence class · unfixable with the data as given; owed to the reader as a limitation

There is no event timestamp, so the evaluation cannot be temporal — but the deployment is

L021 (TabReD) established that when a model predicts forward in time, a random split flatters it: the honest protocol cuts once on time and tests on the future. This submission's deployment memo is explicitly temporal — nightly batch scoring, weekly retraining on a rolling window, labels arriving 4–8 weeks late, monitoring on maturing cohorts. The evaluation behind it is random-in-time, person-grouped 5-fold CV.

So: apply the L021 fix and cut on time. You cannot. Inspect the schema and situations has 59 columns — module_topic, recommended_intervention, context_domain, workload_index, team_size, manager_support_score, season, and 50 opaque ctx_feat_* — and not one of them is an event timestamp. The only date in the whole database is responses.observed_at, which is recorded 4–8 weeks after the event and exists only for labelled rows. A temporal split needs to order rows by when they happened; sorting by when they were observed would order them by a post-outcome quantity and, worse, by one that the report itself suspects correlates with the outcome.

This is a third kind of finding, and it needs its own handling. It is not a bug to fix and not a number to re-measure: it is a declarable limitation plus a request to the data team. Note that the submission is already most of the way there — its question 4 to the data collection team asks exactly for the lag distribution and whether lag correlates with the outcome. What the audit adds is the consequence: until an event timestamp exists, every reported metric is a random-split estimate for a system that will be deployed forward in time, and by TabReD's evidence that estimate is optimistic by an unknown amount. That sentence belongs in the report next to the ship-gate table, not in a backlog.

The triage — an audit's real output

Four findings, and the useful thing about them is that no two want the same response. This table is the deliverable an audit produces; the count of smells is not.

FindingWhat it changesMeasured effectWhat to do
Inner calibration split ignores person_id the artifact; the reported number stays honest log-loss −0.0016, ECE −0.0003 — inside noise fix (one line), keep the measurement as the defence
Winner selected by argmin on 5 correlated folds the decision; every measurement is correct Δ = 8 % of fold σ; winner flips when fold 2 is dropped ship the simpler model (M1); state the tie honestly
Encoder / KMeans / propensity fitted on all rows the reported number — the only class that can be optimistic −0.0011 nats when refitted in-fold: no optimism detectable refit inside the fold for reproducibility; stop reusing "unsupervised ⇒ safe"
No event timestamp ⇒ no temporal split nothing measurable — the caveat you owe the reader unquantifiable with this data declare beside the ship-gate; ask the data team for event time

A fifth observation is not a leakage finding at all, but it is what an audit turns up when it actually runs the code: _lgbm() sets n_jobs=-1, and on a 12-core machine one multiclass fit takes 210 seconds against 9.2 seconds at n_jobs=6 — a 23× penalty from thread contention on 5.6k rows. Nothing about the model changes; the whole 5-model comparison simply becomes cheap enough to rerun, which is what makes re-measuring a finding affordable in the first place. Reproducibility findings are the ones that make every other finding checkable.

Explain it back

This is the load-bearing idea of the lesson — put it in your own words before moving on.

Thesis bridge. Two threads from this audit run straight into the mission. The first is the honest-baseline discipline: the thesis will eventually claim that a relational model beats manual feature engineering, and this lesson is a rehearsal of the standard that claim must meet — a margin of 0.003 nats over five shared folds is not a result, and a graph model that "wins" by that much has won nothing. Everything Years 3–6 report will be judged by the instruments you just used on yourself. The second is subtler and closes a loop back to L035: this pipeline is the flatten. Three tables became one 132-column matrix, and the structure the join discarded is visible in the audit as the thing that keeps causing trouble — person_id is not a feature, it is the surviving trace of a one-to-many relationship, and every finding here except the timestamp is ultimately about failing to respect it somewhere. A model that read the relational entity graph directly would not need a groups= argument threaded correctly through four nested splitters, because the person would be a node rather than a column you must remember to protect.

Where this is subtle (know this too)

  • "Leak" and "inflated metric" are not synonyms. The instinct trained by L002–L005 is that a leak means an optimistic number. Finding 1 is a genuine group leak that inflates nothing, because it happens inside the training fold. Ask "which quantity does this contaminate?" before "how much did it inflate?" — otherwise you will look for the damage in the wrong place and, finding none, conclude there was no defect.
  • Severity is measured, not assumed — in both directions. Finding 1's cost turned out to be a twenty-fifth of a fold's standard deviation. That is a fact about this dataset's label density, not a general truth, and it would have been just as wrong to assume it was large as to assume it was small. An audit that ranks findings by how alarming they sound will misallocate every hour it spends.
  • The most expensive defect can be the one where no number is wrong. Finding 2 breaks no measurement and still put the wrong artifact in production. Leakage checklists do not catch it, because it is a defect of inference, not of data flow. This is why L023's variance discipline and L017's nested CV are part of the same spine as leakage.
  • Your grouping obligation does not end at the split you wrote. The submission's outer CV is asserted correct in a loop. The defect lives in a nested split created by a library from an integer. Audit the splitters you called, not just the ones you constructed: calibration, early stopping, feature selection, and any helper model that manufactures a feature each take a split of their own.
  • "Unsupervised, therefore safe" is a claim about labels only. It is true that no target leaks through an imputer, a scaler, or KMeans. It does not follow that fitting them on all rows is free: that is transduction, and it silently converts your CV estimate into something that is not a deployment estimate. The sentence is not wrong, it is just narrower than the use it gets put to.
  • An audit must be able to return "correct." Seven spine questions passed here, and two of them — the explicit allow-list encoder and the refusal to drop features by mutual information — are better than standard practice. A rubric that always fires is one you will stop running, and crediting the passes is what makes the failures believable to whoever reads the audit.
